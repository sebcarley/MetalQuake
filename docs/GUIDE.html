Quake · a native Metal engine for Apple Silicon

MetalQuake

Quake, 1996, sunk into colder water. The light is traced ray by ray and every shadow belongs to something. The fog pools in the low rooms and hides what is coming. The water goes dark at depth. Between the torches the black is honestly black, and it is not empty. The old game is intact beneath, exactly as id built it — only further from the surface than you remember, and no longer entirely convinced you are alone down there.

MetalQuake is Quake for Apple Silicon: a native Metal renderer, grown out of DarkPlaces, with the maps' real lights traced on the GPU's ray-tracing cores. It runs the original game, the two mission packs, the two later episodes and Arcane Dimensions. It does not change what Quake is. It changes what you can see of it — and what you cannot.

Everything below is on the menus and can be switched off there. The short lists at the end of each part are for the console, for anyone who wants to reach further in; they can be ignored entirely.

I · The light

Every shadow belongs to something

Follow a shadow back far enough and you will find the flame — or whatever is standing between you and the flame.

The light in Quake's halls was painted on. It lies where the mapmaker left it, and nothing that moves has ever disturbed it. Here the light is cast. Every frame, the map's own torches and braziers throw their light through the rooms on the GPU's ray-tracing hardware, and the shadows fall where they must. A pillar's shadow swings across the floor as you pass the torch behind it. A doorway is dark because the wall beside it is in the way. An Ogre coming down the corridor carries its own shadow ahead of it, and you see that before you see the Ogre.

With full wall lighting the ray tracer owns the whole of it. The painted light is set aside and every surface takes exactly the light that reaches it, so a room has a direction — a side that faces the fire and a side that does not — and the corners are as dark as they deserve to be. Light bounces once. Torch glow bleeds round a corner into a room it cannot see directly; lava throws its orange onto the ceiling above it; a brick corridor bleeds brown into its own shadows and a slime hall bleeds green. Whatever is waiting in the corner is lit by what the room is made of.

And the walls are proof against every one of them, not merely the nearest. A room's brightest lamp was once the only one a surface asked about, and the rest shone on regardless — through stone, around corners, out of recesses no eye could find — laying clean bright discs on walls that ought to have been dark. Now a surface puts the question to the brightest few in turn, and a light that cannot see you does not light you.

Small truths follow from that. A torch does not shadow itself. A glowing rune, a lamp face, a lit panel keeps its own light whatever falls across it. The weapon in your hands takes the light of the room it is in: it dims as you walk into the dark, warms beside a torch, flickers with it, and when you fire, the muzzle flash lights the hands holding the gun. The Quad burns blue about you and the Pentagram red, an aura at arm's length rather than a lamp on a pole. Quake marks its dangers in red — the bands on an Ogre's grenade, the buttons, the cross on a health box — and here red is allowed to burn, texel by texel, while brick and dried blood stay as dead as they should. The sky is open sky, so a shaft of light crosses a skylight the way it should. And where a map declares a sun — Arcane Dimensions does — it can be switched on: courtyards and rooftops take real sunlight, with real shadows, and the arcades stay dark.

Lava

Lava boils. Its crust flows across the sheet rather than churning on the spot; it glows, lights the room above it, and warms the air. That warmth is visible: the air over a lava lake shimmers, and whatever you are looking at through it ripples — the far wall, the bridge, Chthon standing in his pit. It is the only warning the game gives before you are standing in it.

At the console rt_metal is the master, rt_metal_walllight the wall lighting, rt_metal_gi the bounce (_emissive and _albedo_tex its lava and colour), rt_metal_glowpass, rt_metal_viewmodel, r_redglow, rt_metal_skyopen. Lava: r_lavaboil, r_lavaflow, r_lavaglow, r_lavashimmer. The sun: exec sun_on.cfg. Menu: Options → RT Shadows.

II · The murk

It lies where the air is stillest

It settles into the low rooms and along the water, and it remembers the shape of the level better than you do.

Not a haze over the screen. The fog knows the level — where the floor is, where the water is, where the corners are — and it settles by that knowledge: into the low rooms, along the waterline, into the crevices, thickest where the air is stillest. A corridor that reads clear at eye level has a knee-deep layer of it lying on the floor, billowing at the top like the surface of a cloud, drifting through the doorway ahead of you as if it had been draining down there for years and you were the first thing to disturb it.

It is lit by the level. A dark room gives dark fog, and a dark passage keeps its dark fog even where it runs beside a bright hall: the light of one room does not leak through the wall into the air of the next. A torch throws a pool of light into it, and inside that pool you can see the fog move. Every light in the room throws its shadow into the fog as well, so a shaft of light through a doorway has the shape of the doorway, and the pillar in the way cuts a dark bar through it. Something crossing that shaft blocks it. You will see the beam go dark before you see what did it.

The fog is kept in three dimensions, so it stays where it is when you turn or step sideways, and it thickens inside the water into something you cannot see through. Dust hangs in the air, and it is ash rather than snow: soot and charcoal, charred brown, a cold blue-grey, and here and there a single pale fleck among them. They drift and eddy around you, take whatever light the room is willing to give, and flare white the instant a rocket goes past. The smoke of an explosion, the blood, the sparks take the room's light and sink into the fog at their proper distance instead of sitting bright in front of it. And the murk holds a warning of its own: the air over lava glows faintly orange from inside, long before the lava is in view.

At the console r_volumetric is the master; _density (keep it low — corners multiply it), _corner, _ground and _groundheight for the floor layer, _ambient for the level's light (_ambientgain its strength, _ambientdilate the wall fill). rt_metal_fog lights it per step; rt_metal_lightsample is every light's shadow in it; rt_metal_fog_froxel the three dimensions. m5_dust 256 for the motes, cl_particles_lighting 2 to light them, cl_particles_lighting_static to settle how much the room lights them without touching what a muzzle flash does. r_volumetric_probe says what the fog sees where you stand. Menu: Options → Volumetric Fog.

III · The water

Nothing is stiller than the water

Stand on the bank and look down. The floor of the pool is there, further off than you thought, and the light on it is not the light you brought.

Quake's water was a sheet. It scrolled, it had a colour, and it hid whatever was under it. This is water. Look down into it from the bank and the floor is there, its stones bending under a surface that moves at the pace of real water — a quick ripple, read from the water's own grain, that catches the light as your view flattens toward the far bank. Directly above a shallow pool you can count the flagstones. From across a moat the pillars run down into a surface you cannot see into. The murk inside it is as thick from the bank as it is once you are under: a shallow pool shows you its bottom, a deep one shows you nothing, and you do not learn which kind you are looking at until you are in it.

A band of mist lies along the waterline. The fog settles onto a lake as it settles onto a floor, level from shore to shore, so a lakeside at night is a cave mouth, a mist lying on black water, and a torch-lit shrine on the far bank reading dimly through it. Where the water is clear it recedes into the fog with distance like everything else, so a pool at the far end of a foggy hall is a darker place in the murk and not a shape cut out of it. It takes the room's light: a torch-lit pool glows, a dark one is black. It does not reflect. The room is not in the water — only what the water hides.

Beneath the surface the murk closes in. How far you can see is a single setting: a hand's reach, or a room's. Slime and lava have their own, so a lava lake is not the same orange haze as a flooded hall, and each has its own colour.

At the console The water needs Force Water Alpha on the Effects and Particles page (r_wateralpha_force 1) with Water Alpha below 1 (r_wateralpha 0.7). r_watersurface is the surface; r_watersurface_clear how much the murk shows from the bank (1 = as thick as from within, 0 = glass). r_volumetric_waterdensity is the murk inside (1.0 is a room's reach, 2.5 a hand's; exec uw_clear.cfg / uw_mid.cfg / uw_thick.cfg), with _slimedensity and _lavadensity beside it; r_volumetric_watermist and _mistheight are the band; rt_metal_liquids the room's light on the surface. exec watersurface_big.cfg to see the mechanism, watersurface_off.cfg for the old sheet.

IV · The lightning gun

The bolt and the ball

The bolt is a straight line drawn between you and what you have decided to kill. The ball is a decision you have let go of.

Hold the trigger and the thunderbolt is a jagged white discharge that re-rolls its shape fifteen times a second, forks in mid-air, frays into hair-thin streamers at every free end and dissolves rather than stopping square. Its edge boils. It lights the room it crosses and the fog it crosses, it is clipped to the stone so it never draws through a wall, and the moment it connects with something it burns brighter and sizzles louder, so you know. The arc wanders between the gun's three electrodes as a real arc does. Its colour is yours to set.

Press 9 and a different gun rises in your hands — the plasma gun from Dissolution of Eternity. It winds up with a rising whine and a globe swelling at the muzzle, and lets go of a sphere of plasma that drifts down the line you aimed at something like a walking pace: a dark sun boiling with plasma, veined blue-white, turning slowly, violet filaments curling off its surface to the glass, dimming what lies behind it, humming. The longer it flies the more violently it heaves, throwing prominences and sparks off itself. Ten times a second it arcs at everything living it can see and sets it alight, and an arc that lands forks on from its victim to the next, so a pack dies together, the core flaring and the filaments thickening like a hand on the glass. It does not hunt. It leans, a little, toward whatever is nearest — more toward a Shambler than a grunt — and goes where you sent it.

When its time is up, or it touches water, it discharges in a blink, a shockwave and a thunderclap, and ignites whatever survives. On a wall it does not explode: it stops there and earths itself, arc after crackling arc into the stone, until the shell collapses. A direct hit disintegrates the globe, and nearly anything it touches. And after one second in the air it no longer knows you. Its drone rises as it comes back within reach, a violet stain creeps in at the edges of your sight, and it will take you as readily as anything else. Fire it, and step back.

At the console r_lightningbeam_m5 is the bolt (Options → Lightning Gun: World Light, Wildness, Branches, Core Hotness, Core Volume, Fog Scatter, the colour); m5_balllightning the ball, key 9 or impulse 202, a row on the M5 Fun Mods page; m5_balllightning_* its speed, life, reach, damage, cost, lean, charge and arming second; r_lightningbeam_m5_ballsize and _ballcolor_* the globe. The bolt's light and the fog's glow need the ray tracer on.

V · The guns and the dead

The old rules bend here

The guns kick, the dead burn, the waves keep coming — and the game beneath does not object. It has seen worse.

The shotgun is a Doom shotgun if you want one: ten pellets, a tighter spread, a harder kick, a warm muzzle flash, and a red hull with a brass head that tumbles out of the breech, bounces on the flagstones and comes to rest. And a gun that fires now flashes, as a gun should: a starburst at the muzzle, following the barrel through its kick and gone in a blink, on your shotguns, nailguns and launchers, on every grunt's gun in the dark ahead of you, and the sparks off a bullet strike are hot needles rather than soft rays. Every spark, ember, puff and drop the engine throws is drawn from one small book of shapes, and that book is printed sixteen times finer, so a spark a yard from your face keeps its edge and its thread instead of dissolving into a soft round glow; an explosion's embers are hot grains with a rim, and blood falls as drops rather than smudges. Smoke and fire wrap the stone instead of being sliced by it: where a puff meets a wall or the floor it thins away rather than ending in a hard line. An explosion's shockwave bends the air: the ring that races out from the blast shows the stone behind it warped outward as it passes. Where the ball earths and where the axe strikes, the scorch is left glowing, an orange that cools to black in a few breaths. The corners keep their dark: the same ray that carries a room's light into its recesses knows how enclosed each point is, and holds the sourceless fill back there, so a corner reads as a corner. The water is no longer blind: the light that the fog carries in the air goes under the surface, so a torch above a pool lays its shafts down into it. And shadows know where their casters stand: hard at a foot on the floor, softening as the caster lifts away, the way a real penumbra does. And the torches live: embers climb out of every flame you can see, drift, cool and die. Under an open sky the storm is let in: a cold flash from above that throws every wall's shadow at once, a second stutter of it, and the thunder arriving a breath later from that quarter of the sky. The air beneath it goes with it — the murk over an open shaft turns pale for that instant and falls dark again — while the air in a closed room, which can see no sky, never moves at all. And the pools have their light: on the stone beneath the water a net of bright cells slides and knots as the surface moves over it. The shotguns spit a spray of sparks that leaves the barrel and travels with it; how many, how far they fly and how long they burn is yours to decide. Lightning ignites. A bolt sets its victim alight — flame, smoke, a charring skin — and the fire is a real light, so a burning Fiend lights the fog around it and throws its own shadow as it comes at you. A Shambler's lightning sets you alight. The Scrag's spit is acid: a yellow-green light rides it, lighting the wall it passes and the fog it passes through, and it sizzles where it lands, on stone or on you.

Explosions have smoke. A white blink, a bang, a shockwave ring that blinks the room's light with it; a fireball with structure; then hot brown smoke rolling up close in and a slower grey plume above it, embers that fall and bounce, a scorch left on the floor. A teleport is a burst of streaks and motes rather than a cloud of dots. The guns feel their work: a snap from the shotgun, the view shoved back by the super shotgun, a tremor from the nailguns, a real shove from the rocket launcher, a buzz from the thunderbolt, a slow heave from the ball gun; nails that stream from both barrels in turn, every third one drawing a hot streak through the fog; an axe that throws sparks off a wall. Each is its own switch, for anyone who would have the old guns back.

The horde. Wave survival on any map — and, if you let it, a horde that reads you. At the start of each wave it weighs your health and armour, the ammunition for the guns you own, how fast you cleared the last wave and how badly it hurt, and shapes the next: rabble and a breather after a wave that nearly had you; more of the heavies, sooner, when you are fat. It is learning what you can take. With notarget on, the monsters forget you and fight each other, and you can stand among them and watch.

Smaller things. Hold a key and time eases down. Freeze the world and fly through it with the HUD gone. Quake 3 air control, or a bunnyhop you can hold. Gore from vanilla to ludicrous. And noclip flies where you look.

The bad torch

A handlamp you can carry: warm tungsten, a soft-edged cone, and thoroughly unreliable. It grumbles constantly, swoons to a third of itself every dozen seconds or so, and about once a minute nearly dies — a second of sullen ember-red before it catches again, reddening as it dims the way a cooling filament does. The beam swings a little behind your view, as if in your hand, and drifts when you stand still. It shows a bulb at its centre, a hot core and a faint dark ring, like a cheap reflector imaging its filament, on the wall and in the fog alike. It can never quite go out. It will simply choose its moment.

At the console Options → M5 Fun Mods is the page: m5_shotgun, m5_burn, m5_venom, m5_kick, m5_grenadebounce, m5_nailtracer (m5_nailtracer_every for how often), m5_nailbarrels, m5_axesparks (exec feel_off.cfg for the old guns), m5_horde and m5_horde_director (impulse 210 next wave, 211 what it made of you), m5_bullettime, m5_photomode, m5_movement, m5_gore, m5_noclipfly. The muzzle flash is on; m5_muzzleflash 0 puts it out, m5_muzzleflash_size for the burst, and m5_muzzleflash_sparks with _speed, _spread, _length, _thickness, _life and _brightness for the shotgun's sparks (exec sparks_reset.cfg puts them back). The finer book of shapes, the drops, the fade against walls, the bending shockwave and the cooling scorch are all on: cl_particles_texsize 64 goes back to the old book (exec cells64.cfg / cells256.cfg), cl_particles_blood_droplet 0 to the old blood, cl_particles_soft 0 to the hard edge (exec soft_off.cfg / soft_on.cfg), cl_particles_refract 0 to the plain ring (exec refract_off.cfg / refract_on.cfg), cl_particles_scorchglow 0 to the dark scorch alone (exec scorchglow_off.cfg / scorchglow_on.cfg), the corners' dark, the light under water and the hardening shadows are on: rt_metal_gi_ao 0 (exec gi_ao_off.cfg / gi_ao_on.cfg), rt_metal_fog_liquidlight 0 (exec fog_liquidlight_off.cfg / _on.cfg), rt_metal_contact 0 (exec contact_off.cfg / contact_on.cfg), the lights keep their own counsel now — rt_metal_shadowlights 1 lets them shine through the walls again (exec spotfix_off.cfg / spotfix_on.cfg), and m5_powerupglow 0 gives the Quad back its plain white lamp (exec quadglow_off.cfg / quadglow_on.cfg), the embers, the storm and the pools' light are on too: m5_torch_embers 0 stills the flames (exec embers_off.cfg / embers_on.cfg), r_skylightning 0 clears the sky, and r_skylightning_fog is how much of a flash reaches the air (exec skylightning_off.cfg / _on.cfg); r_caustics 0 stills the pool floors (exec caustics_off.cfg / caustics_on.cfg). The torch wants a key — Options → Customize Controls, or bind f torch — and m5_torch_cone, _softness and _filament shape it. Explosions and teleports are data in m5/effectinfo.txt; cl_particles_reloadeffects re-reads it live.

VI · Other dimensions

Built by other hands

Beyond the four episodes there are other dimensions, and they were not made for you.

Single Player → Mission Packs opens five more doors: Scourge of Armagon, Dissolution of Eternity, Dimension of the Past, Dimension of the Machine, and Arcane Dimensions. Each takes effect at once, without a restart, and everything engine-side comes with you — the traced light, the murk, the water, the thunderbolt, the higher-poly models and the map textures — because none of it lives in a game directory. Each pack keeps its own lighting and its own fog and is levelled to sit beside Quake's rather than glaring or drowning; the colour its mapper chose is kept.

Arcane Dimensions is a different beast: a total conversion with its own weapons, its own monsters, breakable scenery, secrets, and the largest maps this engine runs. Because it replaces the game's own code, the gameplay mods above stand down inside it; the light, the fog and the lightning do not. Its start map is a hub — walk into a portal. It is also the one place with a sun to switch on.

At the console gamedir m5 hipnotic, rogue, dopa, mg1 or ad does what the menu does. Each pack's few settings live in m5/m5pack_<name>.cfg. In Arcane Dimensions the key inventory is impulse 175. Dimension of the Machine's horde maps load but do not play; that mode lives in the rerelease's own engine.

VII · Seeing, and not

How dark it should be

Quake is a game about not being able to see.

The stock brightness controls never reached the bottom of that. A single Brightness slider does now: at the middle it is the 1996 look, exactly; at a quarter it is moody and playable if you know the map; at fifteen percent it is gloom and inky corners; at nothing it is almost unplayable, and the torches are all there is. It dims the whole finished picture rather than only the lighting, so the fog goes dark with the rooms instead of glowing on its own.

Stand still for a second or two and the view begins to drift — the slow wander the end-of-level camera has always had, as though the thing behind the eye were breathing. Touch anything and it is gone inside a quarter of a second. It moves the view, never your aim.

The light off the leash

On a display running in macOS HDR mode, the game can put a muzzle flash, a lava pool or the core of a bolt on the screen brighter than white — brighter than the HUD's digits, which stay at ordinary white on purpose, so the two side by side in one frame are the proof. One switch does all of it. No screenshot can show it; it has to be judged on the glass.

And the glow around such a thing can be a glow rather than a smear. The old haze took every bright thing and spread it thin; there is a newer bloom that keeps the core hot and lets the light fall away from it across the room, the way a flame reads in a dark hall. It waits at the console until it has been looked at.

At the console Options → Brightness and Gamma; r_brightness is the slider (0.5 is 1996). rt_metal_ambient is the last flat fill holding the blacks up, if you want them dead. v_idlesway is the drift. r_edr 1 is HDR (the HDR (EDR) row on Options → Video Options), r_hdr_shoulder 0.75 rolls the brightest parts into the new range, and r_edr_report says what the display is giving you. r_bloom_m5 1 is the newer bloom (exec bloom_m5_on.cfg; _threshold, _knee, _intensity, _levels shape it).

VIII · Sound

The room answers

You hear the room before you see it.

Quake's samples are the sound, and they play exactly as they always have. What is added is what the room does to them. Every room has a voice: the engine looks around you as you walk — up, down, along the floor — to see how big the space is and how much sky it can find, and the sound answers accordingly. A crypt rings for a couple of seconds after a shot. A corridor is tight. Open ground is nearly dry. It cross-fades as you pass through a door, so you hear the hall before you are in it. Far sounds are wetter than near ones.

A sound with a wall between you and it is dulled, not only turned down, so a Shambler behind a door is a muffled presence and not a distant one. And, if you want it, distance itself dulls: nothing near you changes, but far across an outdoor map an explosion is the low thud you would hear across a valley. Music goes through none of this.

At the console snd_reverb (on; snd_reverb_wet the level, _decay the length), snd_occlusion_lowpass (on; snd_occlusion_cutoff how muffled), snd_airabsorb (off; exec airabsorb_on.cfg). The soundtrack: drop track02.ogg, track03.ogg … into id1/music/; each map asks for its own. bgmvolume is its volume.

IX · Seven ways to run it

From 1996 to the full murk

The machine has a temperature and a mood. These numbers were taken when it had settled into both.

One control, Options → M5 Quality, sets thirty-four things at once and runs from a faithful 1996 to the full murk, with every light shadowed in the fog and every edge filed clean. Every tier but the 1996 one now assembles the picture at a little over a third of the size it is shown at, and — this is the part that matters — traces the light at exactly that size rather than at some other one. The two agreeing is what keeps the silhouettes from climbing; disagreeing was what made them climb, and it cost more, not less. Measured on an M5 at 1080p after the machine had settled, on one heavy scene: the average frame rate, then the worst single second.

Where a roof cuts across the sky, or a near thing stands against a far one, the boundary can climb in small steps rather than lying clean. The instinct is to blame the size the picture is assembled at, and the instinct is wrong. What builds the stair is the light being drawn at one size and laid over a picture drawn at another: the enlargement smears each step across two rows, and a filing tool that looks for a step finds only a slope and leaves it alone. Draw the light at the picture's own size, and file the edges last of all at the size you actually see, and the stair goes. Neither half does it alone — the filing on its own makes the boundary marginally worse — which is why every tier now ships both and there is nothing here to switch on.

r_smaa 1 · rt_metal_scale 1

That filing works by nudging the ends of each tread, and so it reaches a stair whose treads are short and leaves one whose treads are long. There is a second kind, which walks the whole length of a tread before it touches anything and then lays the boundary down across all of it at once; on a clean step it removes the stair almost entirely. It asks one thing in return. A boundary it can measure must be a step and not a smear — and where the light itself is drawn smaller than the room and then enlarged, every edge of light arrives already blurred, and there is nothing left for it to take hold of. Draw the light at full size and the second filing finds its purchase. That costs, as everything that restores a size costs.

r_smaa 1 · rt_metal_scale 1

Six of the seven tiers render the scene smaller than the window and hand the scaling to Apple's MetalFX, which brings the stonework back that plain scaling would soften; the top three use its temporal mode, which accumulates frames and is what stops fog and shadows crawling. The worst-second figures are set by the game's own heavy moments as much as by the tier; on the busiest map, with everything switched off, the worst second still only reaches 176. A cold machine runs a third faster for its first few minutes and then settles; these are the settled numbers.

There is one switch that belongs to no tier. Bind it to a key and the whole of the above falls away at a press — the light, the murk, the bounce, the enlargement, the replacement art at the next map — and 1996 stands in the room instead; press again and everything you had returns exactly as it was. It sets nothing of yours aside for good: what it shows while it is on is never remembered, and a fresh start is always your own picture. A first press after a map has loaded keeps that map’s art until the next one.

bind F6 "toggle m5_cheap" · m5_cheap

At the console r_viewscale is the render size (1 = native), r_metalfx the scaler (0 plain, 1 spatial, 2 temporal), both on Options → Video Options. rt_metal_scale and rt_metal_fog_scale size the shadow and fog buffers as fractions of the window. Set any of them by hand and the tier row reads Custom. m5_cheap is the stock/beautiful switch (never saved).

X · Replay

Watching it back

Nothing in a demo is live, and it will still make you flinch.

The engine can be made to watch itself. The showreel plays a set of demos for their themes and adds the features one at a time, every twenty-five seconds or so — the 1996 picture first, then the light, the murk, the ground fog, the dust, the shadows, the bounce; the fog in motion; the lava; the thunderbolt and the ball; the water; the lake; the sound; and a finale of four action demos each run twice, once at Stock and once at Best, with the frame rate left on screen. Nineteen minutes, no hands on the keys, and it puts your settings back when it is done.

The cinematic capture is a picture no tier can play, for a video. The capture does not run in real time — the engine advances the demo a fixed sixtieth of a second per frame however long each frame takes — so the ray tracer can be set far past any tier: native resolution, thirty-two shadow rays a pixel, the fog on a native buffer with a shadow cast at every step, and the result is the same every time it is run.

At the console exec showreel.cfg, on Metal, sound on. For a capture: exec cinematic.cfg, then playdemo <name>, then cl_capturevideo 1, in that order; the file lands in m5/video/ as an uncompressed AVI (about 180 MB a second at 1080p60 — keep it short, or set cl_capturevideo_width 1280), and exec cinematic_off.cfg before you quit. record <name> records your own demo; playdemo and timedemo play it.

XI · Another Mac

Taking it with you

It travels in two pieces, and neither is any use without the other.

The game is two things that live side by side, in any folder you like — inside Applications, in a Games folder, on an external disk:

QuakeM5.app
packs/id1/ …            your copy of Quake
packs/m5/ …             the mods, models and textures
packs/hipnotic/ …       and rogue, dopa, mg1, ad

packs holds the game data. The app looks for that folder next to itself, so the pair can be moved anywhere together, but the app on its own has nothing to run. To add a mission pack, drop its folder into packs; to remove one, delete it and the menu lists it as absent. The app needs nothing else installed — no Homebrew, no libraries, no command line.

First launch on a new Mac, before anything else. macOS quarantines anything that arrives by download or AirDrop, and that causes both of the failures you might meet: the app refusing to open, or opening and insisting the required files were not found while packs sits right beside it. The second is macOS quietly running a hidden copy of the app from a temporary folder, so it genuinely cannot see its neighbours; the app's own error screen says so when it happens. One Terminal command fixes both, once: open Terminal, paste xattr -dr com.apple.quarantine (with the trailing space), drag the QuakeM5 app onto the Terminal window, press Enter. This is macOS being careful about an app not signed by a paid developer account, not a fault in the build.

A settings file travels in the packs, and a Mac that has never run the game adopts it. The moment you quit, the game writes that Mac's settings to its own home folder, and from then on the shipped copy is ignored: a starting point, not a lock. Screen settings live there too — it asks for 1920×1080 exclusive fullscreen — so on a display that differs, set the resolution again in Options → Video.

At the console Your settings live in ~/Library/Application Support/darkplaces/m5/config.cfg (Finder: Shift-Command-G, paste the path). Quit the game before copying it anywhere; it rewrites the file on exit.

XII · Honestly

What it does not do

Light bounces once here and no further. Some things stay in the dark because the way to them has not been found.

The ray tracer composites over a rasterised frame; it is not a path tracer. Light bounces once: shadowed corners take the colour of the room around them, but a second bounce, caustics and mirrors are all outside what this does. The water refracts and does not reflect — the room is never in it. The sun is an option, and the fog beneath it takes no daylight yet. The fog's map of the level is coarse, so a ledge narrower than a few feet can be missed. The plasma globe is drawn as a sphere on the Metal renderer only.

And nothing here makes the game easier. The light shows you where the shadows fall; it does not show you what is casting them.
