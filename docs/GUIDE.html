Quake · a native Metal engine for Apple Silicon

MetalQuake

Quake, 1996, sunk into colder water. The light is traced ray by ray, and every shadow belongs to something. The fog pools in the low rooms and hides what is coming. The water goes dark at depth. Between the torches the black is honestly black, and it is not empty. The old game lies intact beneath, exactly as id built it — only further from the surface, and no longer alone.

MetalQuake is Quake for Apple Silicon: a native Metal renderer, grown out of DarkPlaces, with the maps' own lights traced on the GPU's ray-tracing cores. It runs the original game, the two mission packs, the two later episodes and Arcane Dimensions. It does not change what Quake is. It changes what you can see of it — and what you cannot.

Each part below opens with what the thing is, then goes deeper. Everything is on the menus and can be switched off there. The table at the foot of each part is for the console, for anyone who would reach further in; it can be passed over entirely.

I · The light

Every shadow belongs to something

Follow a shadow back far enough and you will find the flame — or whatever stands between you and the flame.

The map's own torches, braziers and lava cast their light through the rooms every frame, on the GPU's ray-tracing hardware. Shadows fall where they must. Light bounces once. Nothing painted onto the stone in 1996 is trusted over what the flame can actually reach.

Cast light

A pillar's shadow swings across the floor as you pass the torch behind it. A doorway is dark because the wall beside it is in the way. An Ogre coming down the corridor carries its own shadow ahead of it, and you see the shadow before you see the Ogre. Each surface takes its light from the brightest few lamps that can see it — through no wall, round no corner — and a light that cannot see you does not light you.

Full wall lighting, and the single bounce

With full wall lighting the ray tracer owns the whole of it. The painted light is set aside and every surface takes exactly the light that reaches it, so a room has a direction — a side that faces the fire and a side that does not — and the corners are as dark as they deserve to be. Light bounces once: torch glow bleeds round a corner into a room it cannot see directly; lava throws its orange onto the ceiling above it; a brick corridor bleeds brown into its own shadows and a slime hall bleeds green. Whatever waits in the corner is lit by what the room is made of.

The same ray knows how enclosed each point of the room is, and holds the last thin fill back from the recesses, so a corner reads as a corner. Shadows know where their casters stand: hard at a foot on the floor, softening as the caster lifts away, the way a real penumbra does. A final sourceless fill keeps the blacks from dying altogether; it can be taken to nothing.

Small truths

A torch does not shadow itself. A glowing rune, a lamp face, a lit panel keeps its own light whatever falls across it. Embers climb out of every flame you can see, drift, cool and die. The weapon in your hands takes the light of the room it is in — dims as you walk into the dark, warms beside a torch, flickers with it — and when you fire, the muzzle flash lights the hands holding the gun. The Quad burns blue about you and the Pentagram red, an aura at arm's length rather than a lamp on a pole. Quake marks its dangers in red — the bands on an Ogre's grenade, the buttons, the cross on a health box — and red is allowed to burn, texel by texel, while brick and dried blood stay as dead as they should.

The sky is open sky, so a shaft of light crosses a skylight as it should, and under an open sky the storm is let in: a cold flash from above that throws every wall's shadow at once, a second stutter of it, and the thunder arriving a breath later from that quarter of the sky. The murk over an open shaft turns pale for that instant and falls dark again; the air in a closed room, which can see no sky, never moves at all. Where a map declares a sun — Arcane Dimensions does — it can be switched on: courtyards and rooftops take real sunlight with real shadows, and the arcades stay dark.

Lava

Lava boils. Its crust flows across the sheet rather than churning on the spot; it glows, lights the room above it, and warms the air. That warmth is visible: the air over a lava lake shimmers, and whatever you are looking at through it ripples — the far wall, the bridge, Chthon standing in his pit. It is the only warning the game gives before you are standing in it.

II · The murk

It lies where the air is stillest

It settles into the low rooms and along the water, and it remembers the shape of the level better than you do.

The fog knows the level — where the floor is, where the water is, where the corners are — and settles by that knowledge. It is lit by the room it lies in, every light throws its shadow into it, and it is kept in three dimensions, so it stays put when you turn.

Where it settles

Not a haze over the screen. It gathers in the low rooms, along the waterline, into the crevices, thickest where the air is stillest. A corridor that reads clear at eye level has a knee-deep layer of it lying on the floor, billowing at the top like the surface of a cloud, drifting through the doorway ahead of you as though it had been draining down there for years and you were the first thing to disturb it.

Lit by the level

A dark room gives dark fog, and a dark passage keeps its dark fog where it runs beside a bright hall: the light of one room does not leak through the wall into the air of the next. A torch throws a pool of light into it, and inside that pool you can see the fog move. Every light in the room throws its shadow into the fog as well, so a shaft of light through a doorway has the shape of the doorway, and the pillar in the way cuts a dark bar through it. Something crossing that shaft blocks it. You will see the beam go dark before you see what did it.

Held in three dimensions

The fog occupies the room rather than the screen, so it stays where it is when you turn or step sideways, and it thickens inside the water into something you cannot see through. The smoke of an explosion, the blood and the sparks take the room's light and sink into the fog at their proper distance instead of sitting bright in front of it. The murk holds a warning of its own: the air over lava glows faintly orange from inside, long before the lava is in view.

Ash

Dust hangs in the air, and it is ash rather than snow: soot and charcoal, charred brown, a cold blue-grey, and here and there a single pale fleck among them. The motes drift and eddy around you, take whatever light the room is willing to give, and flare white the instant a rocket goes past.

III · The water

Nothing is stiller than the water

Stand on the bank and look down. The floor of the pool is there, further off than you thought, and the light on it is not the light you brought.

Water is water. It has a surface that moves at the pace of real water, a floor you can see through to when the pool is shallow, a murk that closes in at depth, and a band of mist along its edge. It takes the room's light. It does not reflect the room.

The surface

Look down into it from the bank and the floor is there, its stones bending under a surface that moves at the pace of real water — a quick ripple, read from the water's own grain, that catches the light as your view flattens toward the far bank. Directly above a shallow pool you can count the flagstones. From across a moat the pillars run down into a surface you cannot see into. The murk inside it is as thick from the bank as it is once you are under: a shallow pool shows you its bottom, a deep one shows you nothing, and you do not learn which kind you are looking at until you are in it.

The waterline

A band of mist lies along the water. The fog settles onto a lake as it settles onto a floor, level from shore to shore, so a lakeside at night is a cave mouth, a mist lying on black water, and a torch-lit shrine on the far bank reading dimly through it. Where the water is clear it recedes into the fog with distance like everything else, so a pool at the far end of a foggy hall is a darker place in the murk and not a shape cut out of it. A torch-lit pool glows; a dark one is black. The room is never in the water — only what the water hides.

Beneath

Under the surface the murk closes in. How far you can see is a single setting: a hand's reach, or a room's. Slime and lava have their own, so a lava lake is not the same orange haze as a flooded hall, and each has its own colour. The light the fog carries in the air goes under the surface with you, so a torch above a pool lays its shafts down into it. And on the stone beneath the water a net of bright cells slides and knots as the surface moves over it.

IV · The lightning gun

The bolt and the ball

The bolt is a straight line drawn between you and what you have decided to kill. The ball is a decision you have let go of.

The thunderbolt is a living discharge that lights the room and the fog it crosses. Press 9 and a second gun rises: the plasma gun from Dissolution of Eternity, which lets go of a slow, humming globe that arcs at everything living it can see — and, after a second, at you.

The bolt

Hold the trigger and the thunderbolt is a jagged white discharge that re-rolls its shape fifteen times a second, forks in mid-air, frays into hair-thin streamers at every free end and dissolves rather than stopping square. Its edge boils. It lights the room it crosses and the fog it crosses, it is clipped to the stone so it never draws through a wall, and the moment it connects with something it burns brighter and sizzles louder, so you know. The arc wanders between the gun's three electrodes as a real arc does. Its colour is yours to set.

The ball

The plasma gun winds up with a rising whine and a globe swelling at the muzzle, and lets go of a sphere of plasma that drifts down the line you aimed at something like walking pace: a dark sun boiling with plasma, veined blue-white, turning slowly, violet filaments curling off its surface to the glass, dimming what lies behind it, humming. The longer it flies the more violently it heaves, throwing prominences and sparks off itself. Ten times a second it arcs at everything living it can see and sets it alight, and an arc that lands forks on from its victim to the next, so a pack dies together, the core flaring and the filaments thickening like a hand on the glass. It does not hunt. It leans, a little, toward whatever is nearest — more toward a Shambler than a grunt — and goes where you sent it.

When its time is up, or it touches water, it discharges in a blink, a shockwave and a thunderclap, and ignites whatever survives. On a wall it does not explode: it stops there and earths itself, arc after crackling arc into the stone, until the shell collapses. A direct hit disintegrates the globe, and nearly anything it touches. And after one second in the air it no longer knows you. Its drone rises as it comes back within reach, a violet stain creeps in at the edges of your sight, and it will take you as readily as anything else. Fire it, and step back.

V · The arsenal and the dead

The old rules bend here

The guns kick, the dead burn, the waves keep coming — and the game beneath does not object. It has seen worse.

A set of gameplay mods, each its own switch, on Options → M5 Fun Mods: a Doom shotgun, guns that flash and kick, lightning that sets things alight, acid that sizzles, explosions with smoke, a horde mode that studies you, and a handlamp that cannot be trusted.

The shotgun, and the flash

The shotgun is a Doom shotgun if you want one: ten pellets, a tighter spread, a harder kick, a warm muzzle flash, and a red hull with a brass head that tumbles out of the breech, bounces on the flagstones and comes to rest. A gun that fires flashes: a starburst at the muzzle, following the barrel through its kick and gone in a blink, on your shotguns, nailguns and launchers, and on every grunt's gun in the dark ahead of you. The shotguns spit a spray of sparks that leaves the barrel and travels with it; how many, how far they fly and how long they burn is yours to decide.

The book of shapes

Every spark, ember, puff and drop the engine throws is drawn from one small book of shapes, and that book is printed sixteen times finer than Quake's own, so a spark a yard from your face keeps its edge and its thread instead of dissolving into a soft round glow. An explosion's embers are hot grains with a rim; blood falls as drops rather than smudges; the sparks off a bullet strike are hot needles rather than soft rays. Smoke and fire wrap the stone instead of being sliced by it: where a puff meets a wall or the floor it thins away rather than ending in a hard line. An explosion's shockwave bends the air, the stone behind the ring warped outward as it passes. Where the ball earths and where the axe strikes, the scorch is left glowing, an orange that cools to black in a few breaths.

Fire and venom

Lightning ignites. A bolt sets its victim alight — flame, smoke, a charring skin — and the fire is a real light, so a burning Fiend lights the fog around it and throws its own shadow as it comes at you. A Shambler's lightning sets you alight. The Scrag's spit is acid: a yellow-green light rides it, lighting the wall it passes and the fog it passes through, and it sizzles where it lands, on stone or on you.

Explosions, and the feel of the guns

An explosion is a white blink, a bang, a shockwave ring that blinks the room's light with it; a fireball with structure; then hot brown smoke rolling up close in and a slower grey plume above it, embers that fall and bounce, a scorch left on the floor. A teleport is a burst of streaks and motes rather than a cloud of dots. The guns feel their work: a snap from the shotgun, the view shoved back by the super shotgun, a tremor from the nailguns, a real shove from the rocket launcher, a buzz from the thunderbolt, a slow heave from the ball gun. Nails stream from both barrels in turn, every third one drawing a hot streak through the fog, and an axe throws sparks off a wall. Each is its own switch, for anyone who would have the old guns back.

The horde

Wave survival on any map — and, if you let it, a horde that reads you. At the start of each wave it weighs your health and armour, the ammunition for the guns you own, how fast you cleared the last wave and how badly it hurt, and shapes the next: rabble and a breather after a wave that nearly had you; more of the heavies, sooner, when you are fat. It is learning what you can take. With notarget on, the monsters forget you and fight each other, and you can stand among them and watch.

Smaller things

Hold a key and time eases down. Freeze the world and fly through it with the HUD gone. Quake 3 air control, or a bunnyhop you can hold. Gore from vanilla to ludicrous. Noclip flies where you look.

The bad torch

A handlamp you can carry: warm tungsten, a soft-edged cone, and thoroughly unreliable. It grumbles constantly, swoons to a third of itself every dozen seconds or so, and about once a minute nearly dies — a second of sullen ember-red before it catches again, reddening as it dims the way a cooling filament does. The beam swings a little behind your view, as if in your hand, and drifts when you stand still. It shows a bulb at its centre, a hot core and a faint dark ring, like a cheap reflector imaging its filament, on the wall and in the fog alike. It can never quite go out. It will simply choose its moment.

VI · Other dimensions

Built by other hands

Beyond the four episodes there are other dimensions, and they were not made for you.

Single Player → Mission Packs opens five more doors — Scourge of Armagon, Dissolution of Eternity, Dimension of the Past, Dimension of the Machine, and Arcane Dimensions. Each takes effect at once, without a restart, and everything engine-side comes with you.

The traced light, the murk, the water, the thunderbolt, the higher-poly models and the map textures follow you into every pack, because none of it lives in a game directory. Each pack keeps its own lighting and its own fog, levelled to sit beside Quake's rather than glaring or drowning, and the colour its mapper chose is kept.

Arcane Dimensions

A different beast: a total conversion with its own weapons, its own monsters, breakable scenery, secrets, and the largest maps this engine runs. Because it replaces the game's own code, the gameplay mods of Part V stand down inside it; the light, the fog and the lightning do not. Its start map is a hub — walk into a portal. It is also the one place with a sun to switch on.

VII · Seeing, and not

How dark it should be

Quake is a game about not being able to see.

One Brightness slider reaches from the 1996 look to a black the torches barely hold back. The view breathes when you stand still. On an HDR display the fire is brighter than white, and the glow around it is a glow rather than a smear.

Brightness

At the middle of the slider it is the 1996 look, exactly; at a quarter it is moody and playable if you know the map; at fifteen percent it is gloom and inky corners; at nothing it is almost unplayable, and the torches are all there is. It dims the whole finished picture rather than only the lighting, so the fog goes dark with the rooms instead of glowing on its own.

The breath behind the eye

Stand still for a second or two and the view begins to drift — the slow wander the end-of-level camera has always had, as though the thing behind the eye were breathing. Touch anything and it is gone inside a quarter of a second. It moves the view, never your aim.

The light off the leash

On a display running in macOS HDR mode, a muzzle flash, a lava pool or the core of a bolt can be put on the screen brighter than white — brighter than the HUD's digits, which stay at ordinary white on purpose, so the two side by side in one frame are the proof. One switch does all of it. No screenshot can show it; it has to be judged on the glass.

The glow around such a thing can be a glow rather than a smear: a bloom that keeps the core hot and lets the light fall away from it across the room, the way a flame reads in a dark hall. It waits at the console.

VIII · Sound

The room answers

You hear the room before you see it.

Quake's samples are the sound, and they play exactly as they always have. What is added is what the room does to them: every space has a voice, walls dull what stands behind them, and, if you want it, distance dulls too.

The voice of the room

The engine looks around you as you walk — up, down, along the floor — to see how big the space is and how much sky it can find, and the sound answers accordingly. A crypt rings for a couple of seconds after a shot. A corridor is tight. Open ground is nearly dry. It cross-fades as you pass through a door, so you hear the hall before you are in it. Far sounds are wetter than near ones.

What the wall keeps back

A sound with a wall between you and it is dulled, not only turned down, so a Shambler behind a door is a muffled presence and not a distant one. With the air's own absorption on, nothing near you changes, but far across an outdoor map an explosion is the low thud you would hear across a valley. Music goes through none of this.

IX · Seven ways to run it

From 1996 to the full murk

The machine has a temperature and a mood. These numbers were taken when it had settled into both.

One control, Options → M5 Quality, sets thirty-four things at once and runs from a faithful 1996 to the full murk, with every light shadowed in the fog and every edge filed clean. Every tier but the first assembles the picture at a little over a third of its shown size, traces the light at that same size, and files the edges last, at the size you see.

Measured on an M5 at 1080p after the machine had settled, on one heavy scene: the average frame rate, then the worst single second.

TierAverageWorst secondWhat it is

Superfast149–15992–96The traced light and the fog on the smallest buffers, enlarged by the simpler of the two upscalers; no per-light shadowing in the fog, no bounce light.
Fast148–14993–96As Superfast, with every light casting its shadow into the fog; no bounce light, and a flat memory of the frame before.
Good144–14594–95The fog on a larger buffer with its full kernel, still remembering only a flat image of the frame before; no bounce light.
Better112–11373–76The fog becomes a volume that remembers itself in three dimensions, and the enlargement becomes the one that accumulates many frames — the largest single step in the ladder, and where the murk stops crawling; no bounce light.
Best102–10355–69The reference look. All of Better, plus light that bounces once off every surface before it reaches you, so a shadowed corner is lit by the wall beside it rather than painted dark.
Ultimate99–10066–68Best with the fog given the larger of its two buffers. The last step there is.

Six of the seven tiers render the scene smaller than the window and hand the scaling to Apple's MetalFX, which brings the stonework back that plain scaling would soften; the top three use its temporal mode, which accumulates frames and is what stops fog and shadows crawling. The worst-second figures are set by the game's own heavy moments as much as by the tier; on the busiest map, with everything switched off, the worst second still only reaches 176. A cold machine runs a third faster for its first few minutes and then settles; these are the settled numbers.

The switch that belongs to no tier

Bind it to a key and the whole of the above falls away at a press — the light, the murk, the bounce, the enlargement, the replacement art at the next map — and 1996 stands in the room instead. Press again and everything you had returns exactly as it was. What it shows while it is on is never saved; a fresh start is always your own picture. A first press after a map has loaded keeps that map's art until the next one.

X · Replay

Watching it back

Nothing in a demo is live, and it will still make you flinch.

The engine can be made to watch itself: a nineteen-minute showreel that adds the features one at a time, and a cinematic capture that renders a picture no tier can play, for a video.

The showreel

A set of demos plays for its themes, adding a feature every twenty-five seconds or so — the 1996 picture first, then the light, the murk, the ground fog, the dust, the shadows, the bounce; the fog in motion; the lava; the thunderbolt and the ball; the water; the lake; the sound; and a finale of four action demos each run twice, once at Stock and once at Best, with the frame rate left on screen. No hands on the keys, and it puts your settings back when it is done.

The cinematic capture

The capture does not run in real time. The engine advances the demo a fixed sixtieth of a second per frame however long each frame takes, so the ray tracer can be set far past any tier — native resolution, thirty-two shadow rays a pixel, the fog on a native buffer with a shadow cast at every step — and the result is the same every time it is run.

packs/hipnotic/   … and rogue, dopa, mg1, ad

The app looks for packs next to itself, so the pair can be moved anywhere together — Applications, a Games folder, an external disk — but the app on its own has nothing to run. To add a mission pack, drop its folder into packs; to remove one, delete it, and the menu lists it as absent. No Homebrew, no libraries, no command line.

The textures and most of the models are already in packs/m5. The models that pack drew from the commercial remaster are not, since only their owners may hand them on: the Ogre, the Fiend, the Scrag and their kin arrive as id made them until you fetch that pack yourself and drop its models in beside the others.

First launch on a new Mac

Before anything else, once: open Terminal, paste xattr -dr com.apple.quarantine (with the trailing space), drag the MetalQuake app onto the Terminal window, press Enter. Until that is done, macOS's quarantine either refuses to open the app or runs a hidden copy of it from a temporary folder, where it cannot see packs beside it and says so on its own error screen.

Settings

A settings file travels in the packs, and a Mac that has never run the game adopts it. On quitting, the game writes that Mac's own settings to its home folder, and from then on the shipped copy is a starting point, not a lock. Screen settings live there too — it asks for 1920×1080 exclusive fullscreen — so on a display that differs, set the resolution again in Options → Video.

XII · The edge of the known

What lies beyond the light

Light bounces once here and no further. Some things stay in the dark because the way to them has not been found.

The ray tracer composites over a rasterised frame; it is not a path tracer. Light bounces once. A second bounce, caustics from lamps, and mirrors are outside what it does. The water refracts and does not reflect — the room is never in it. The sun is an option, and the fog beneath it takes no daylight. The fog's map of the level is coarse, so a ledge narrower than a few feet can be missed. The plasma globe is drawn as a sphere on the Metal renderer only.

And nothing here makes the game easier. The light shows you where the shadows fall; it does not show you what is casting them.
