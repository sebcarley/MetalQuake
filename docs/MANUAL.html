MetalQuake

Quake, 1996, sunk into colder water. The light is traced ray by ray and the shadows answer to it; the fog pools in the low rooms and keeps its own counsel; the water bends what lies beneath it and goes dark at depth; between the torches, the black is honestly black. The old game is intact beneath — rock-solid, exactly as id built it — just further from the surface than you remember, and no longer entirely convinced you are alone down there.

MetalQuake is a native Metal engine for Apple Silicon. It grew out of DarkPlaces and has long since become its own thing: the renderer is Metal, the maps' real lights are traced on the GPU's ray-tracing cores, and on top of the 1996 game sit four things — ray-traced light, fog that settles into rooms rather than hazing the screen, water that bends what lies beneath it, and a lightning gun with two faces — with a set of gameplay mods beside them.

Everything answers to a console variable, and every variable is documented in SETTINGS.md. The menus carry the ones worth reaching for; this guide is the tour of what they do, why, and where in the game to go and stand to see them.

I · Light

Ray tracing

Every shadow in these halls belongs to something. Follow it back far enough and you will find the flame — or whatever is standing between you and the flame.

The torches in the Necropolis were burning long before you came down the stairs, and for thirty years the walls were never asked what they showed. A ray tracer runs inside the renderer now, and it asks. Every frame it traces the map's real lights on the GPU's ray-tracing hardware and lays the answer over the scene: shadows that move when the light moves, torches that genuinely light the stone near them, and a black behind every pillar that is there because something is in the way.

rt_metal is the master, and Options → RT Shadows (Metal) is its page.

rt_metal_samples · _softness · _darkness
Soft shadows. More samples give smoother penumbrae; softness is how large an area each light pretends to be; darkness is how black a fully shadowed surface goes.
rt_metal_walllight
The big one. Above 0, the ray tracer computes all the lighting rather than layering shadows over Quake's baked lightmaps. Rooms gain real directionality and every shadow belongs to a light you can find.
rt_metal_lightcap
On by default at 0.75. A ceiling on how bright any one map light may be uploaded. Most Quake lights are 150–300; e1m2's water hall has three at 800, and under wall lighting one of those pins every wall near it flat white — the overbright corridor. At 0.75 those lights come down to a strong torch's level while ordinary ones barely move; the far walls of that hall get their brickwork back, though the hall stays bright — it is also simply full of lights, and only the global rt_metal_lmax knee reaches that. 1 is gentler, 0 removes the ceiling. It changes nothing on a map without such lights.
rt_metal_lmax
The knee, at 2.5. A surface beside its own light is lit five to seven times over, and under HDR its texture detail blows past white for the upscaler to sharpen into bright single pixels along a monster's edge or round a wall fixture. This rolls the lighting off softly above the knee; 0 is the old unbounded look, 2 a flatter one.
rt_metal_fog
Moves the entire fog calculation into the ray tracer, so the fog is lit per step along the view ray. This is what produces god rays: light shafts with genuine occlusion structure, because each sample fires a shadow ray at the light.
r_lavaboil · r_lavaflow · r_lavaglow · r_lavashimmer · rt_metal_lavalights
Lava boils, glows, lights the room above it, and warms the air around it. Since r_lavaflow it also flows — the crust drifts across the sheet instead of only churning on the spot, which is what stops a big lake reading as an animated texture. r_lavaflow_speed sets the pace, and r_volumetric_mistlavacut keeps the surface mist off it, since cold mist on molten rock never looked right.
And the air above it shimmers — r_lavashimmer refracts whatever you can see through that rising heat, which is the only warning the game gives you before you are standing in it. Think of a photograph of a bonfire: what bends is the building behind the flames, not the flames. So it is the air between your eye and what you are looking at that counts — a wall on the far side of a lava lake ripples, and so does Chthon standing in one. ..._height sets how far the lava heats the air around it, ..._path how much of that air you have to be looking through for the full effect, and ..._dist how far away a surface can be and still ripple.
r_teleportswirl · r_teleportswirl_pivot · r_teleportswirl_churn
Teleporter starfields boil under the same noise the water churns to. They used to rotate, and about a point near the map's origin rather than about the pad — so how fast a starfield moved depended on where in the level the teleporter happened to be built. On the start map alone the three skill pads ran at 121, 271 and 439 pixels a second, and across a whole map the spread was over tenfold; it also swamped the churn about thirty to one, which is why you could see the starfield was distorted but never saw it change. The rotation is off (_pivot 2) and the churn does the work at eight times its old rate: the field visibly boils, at the same pace on every pad in every map. _pivot 0 with _churn 1 puts the old behaviour back exactly; 1 keeps a slow rotation but shows a faint seam where texture tiles meet.
r_redglow
Quake marks danger in saturated red — the bands on an Ogre's grenades, the buttons, the health-box cross — and almost none of it was ever allowed to glow. This makes red burn: a red enough texel emits its own colour, judged texel by texel, so brickwork, brown shadow and dried blood stay as dead as they should be.
rt_metal_sameframe
Normally the ray tracer runs a frame ahead of what you see, and its result is warped to match your current view — nearly perfect, but close edges and monsters can show a faint one-frame fringe when you move. This traces and shows the same frame instead: no fringe, at a cost that ranges from nothing when the GPU is already busy to roughly an eighth of your frame rate. On by default. There is no menu row for it — rt_metal_sameframe 0 at the console turns it off.

Torch flames are lifted out of the shadow-caster set into their own emissive pass. Without that they blocked their own light and traced to black — which is what a torch model sitting exactly on its light source will do.

In the fog, every light casts a shadow. Each fog sample sends its one shadow ray to a light chosen in proportion to what that light actually delivers there, so over a handful of frames every light — torches included — shadows correctly, fog gains real shape around its lights, and god rays carry genuine per-light structure. Torch glow no longer spills through walls into fog it cannot see. That per-light structure is the fog's “detail and clumpiness”; without it the flat fill reads as a haze, and it was sent straight back the one afternoon it was tried without. Every tier from Fast up carries the pick; only Stock and Superfast leave it out. rt_metal_lightsample 0 restores the old dominant-light-only fog exactly. On surfaces (walls, monsters) the brightest light at each point still takes the shadow ray and the rest are added unshadowed — the stochastic version of that (rt_metal_lightsample 2) sparkled in motion and stays an A/B curiosity only.

Rooms are lit by bounce light. One-bounce global illumination (Bounce Lighting on the RT Shadows page, rt_metal_gi, on by default) fires a bounce ray from every surface the ray tracer shades and gathers the real lights at the point it lands, so torch glow bleeds into shadowed corners and a room reads lit rather than painted. It needs wall lighting above 0 and costs a little under a millisecond of GPU — a few frames per second at the Best tier, rather more at Good, so Best and Ultimate have it on and Superfast, Fast, Good and Better leave it off (Better lost it in the 19 September retier, where it became the Better → Best rung and measured ×1.10 on demo48). 0 restores the direct-light-only look exactly. Lava and torch flames bounce their own glow too (rt_metal_gi_emissive, on at 0.65) — lava throws orange onto the walls and ceilings above it — and the bounce carries each surface's own colour (rt_metal_gi_albedo_tex, on), so a brick corridor bleeds brown into its corners and a slime hall bleeds green. 0 on either switches just that part off.

The same principle covers every glowing surface: a lamp face, a glowing panel or rune emits its own light, so wall lighting must not shade it. Its glow is re-added at authored brightness after the ray-traced lighting is applied (rt_metal_glowpass), which is why a recessed slot lamp no longer renders extinguished when its lighting term happens to be in shadow. 0 restores the old behaviour exactly.

Your weapon is lit by the room. Wall lighting renders the world unlit and paints the ray-traced light back over it — but that repaint is deliberately masked off the weapon in your hands (otherwise it would be shaded by whatever wall happens to be behind it), so for a while the gun sat at one flat brightness in every room, and the muzzle flash lit everything except the hands holding it. It takes its light from the same lights the ray tracer uses now (Weapon Lighting on the RT Shadows page): it dims as you walk into the dark, warms beside a torch, flickers with it, catches your own lamp, and flares when you fire. A few of the brightest lights are properly occlusion-tested, so stepping behind a pillar dims it rather than leaving it lit through the stone. Measured on E1M3: the gun used to read the same brightness in a lit hall and a dark corner; it now spans more than six to one between them. 0 restores the old flat weapon exactly.

Sky counts as open sky to the ray tracer rather than as a wall (rt_metal_skyopen), so wall lighting no longer darkens patches of a skylight into black boxes, and god-ray beams genuinely cross sky windows. The fog keeps a cap over sky too (r_volumetric_skyfog, default 0.8): a fogged skylight reads as sky seen through fog, never fog instead of sky. Both at 0/1 restore the old behaviour exactly.

The sun is an option, not a default — your word: it is well executed and subtle, and it does not fit lots of maps. exec sun_on.cfg gives the ray tracer a sun. Where a map declares one (Arcane Dimensions does: its start hub, the Sepulcher and its first level) every surface casts a ray toward it and is lit wherever that ray reaches open sky, so courtyards and rooftops take real sunlight with real shadows; the fog beneath takes none yet. Stock Quake declares no sun; exec sun_e1m1.cfg sets one by hand over e1m1's outdoor start, and rt_metal_sun_mangle / rt_metal_sun_light are the knobs for any other map. It only shows under Full Wall Lighting, where the map's baked sun is thrown away with the rest of the lightmap. exec sun_off.cfg reverts.

Where to look

The first corridor of e1m3, the Necropolis, past the three zombies in their vault: the torches on the walls light the stone near them and nothing else. Then e1m7, the House of Chthon — stand at the top of the stair and watch the lava throw orange onto the ceiling, and the far wall ripple through the heat.

II · The murk

Volumetric fog

It lies where the floor is lowest and the air is stillest, and it remembers the shape of the room better than you do.

Not screen haze. A map of the level is baked as the level loads — where the floor is, where the water is, where the corners are — and the fog settles by it: into the low rooms, along the waterline, into the crevices, as if it had been draining down there for years and you were the first thing to disturb it.

r_volumetric is the master; the rest is on Options → Volumetric Fog.

r_volumetric_density · _corner
Density is the open air value and wants to be low — corner gathering multiplies it up several times in corners and crevices, which is where fog belongs.
r_volumetric_ambient
On by default: the fog is lit by the level rather than by itself. A dark room gives dark fog, a torch-lit doorway throws a visible pool into it, and the ground mist stops glowing on its own. _ambientgain (1) is the strength if the fog reads too dark or too bright against its room; 0 restores the old self-lit murk. The light grid fills the cells inside walls with the average of the open air beside them (_ambientdilate 1; 0 is the older brightest-neighbour fill, under which most of the air read fully lit) on 64-unit cells (r_volumetric_irrcell). With the ray-traced fog on, most of what the fog shows is the light the lamps and torches scatter into it; this term sets how dark the fog's own body is beneath that.
r_volumetric_noise2
On by default: the fog's structure is baked from folded, warped clumps rather than blobs on a grid, and every map gets its own pattern. 0 restores the classic noise exactly.
r_volumetric_swirl
On by default: the fog rolls and shears as it drifts — banks curl instead of sliding past as one sheet, and the top of the ground mist billows where it meets the clear air, like cloud tops. The value is how far the fog is stirred, in world units; 0 restores the straight drift exactly.
r_volumetric_ground · _groundheight · _groundcolour
A shallow dry-ice layer hugging each room's floor, with its own billow and drift. Set the colour to near-black for something inky rather than misty.
rt_metal_fog_froxel
On by default, and on every tier from Good up: the fog is kept in three dimensions rather than as a flat picture. The fog kernel marches fixed depth slices and each slice remembers its own history at its own distance, so the smoothing can run ten frames deep without trailing the scene when you strafe — the trailing a flat history cannot avoid, which is why deeper flat histories were tried and parked. Your word on first sight: “froxel looks amazing, keep that.” Lit fog is calmer walking and turning; a light flick or a door settles within a third of a second. Fast keeps the old flat history on its small fog buffer until a bench prices the volume there. exec froxel_off.cfg is the flat history for A/B; froxel24.cfg is a cheaper 24-slice arm that reads a touch brighter than the true fog, and froxel095.cfg a deeper history.
r_volumetric_water · _waterdensity
Thickens and tints the murk inside water, slime and lava, each with its own colour and its own density. Set air density to 0 and this alone gives you clear air with murky pools. The water has its own chapter below, and the murk inside it is most of that chapter.
r_volumetric_particles
Fades blood, gibs, smoke and explosions into the fog by distance. Without it they read bright and clear through fog thick enough to hide the wall behind them, because the fog is drawn before they are. The fade works by thinning the particle rather than recolouring it, so a dark smoke puff at the far end of a foggy hall is shrouded by the fog exactly as the wall is, instead of sitting black in front of it; r_volumetric_particles_alpha 0 is the old recolouring.
cl_particles_lighting
Off by default. Every particle Quake has ever drawn was unlit — smoke over a torch and smoke in a pitch-black corridor were the same grey. Set this to 2 and smoke, dust and the like take the room's light: they read from the same baked lighting grid the fog reads, so they sit in the fog as if they belonged to the same air, and a muzzle flash, a rocket's glow or the handlamp lights them as it passes. 1 is the engine's own exact per-particle lookup with a shadow test per light, which costs about four times as much and shows only where a wall is between a flash and its smoke. cl_particles_lighting_gain is the brightness knob if the smoke reads too dark or too bright against the walls.
cl_particles_texsize
256 by default since Seb's eye passed the round, or 64 for the old atlas. Every particle the engine draws — sparks, embers, smoke puffs, the muzzle flash, the shell casing, a drop of blood — is a cell of one generated atlas, and that atlas has been 64 pixels a cell since 2001, so a spark magnified across a hundred pixels of the screen is a gaussian smear whatever shape it was meant to be. At 256 the atlas is 2048 pixels square (16 MB) and every cell is authored sixteen times finer: the flash keeps its tongues, a streak keeps its thread, an ember keeps its rim, and the smoke puffs carry two more octaves of detail for free. It regenerates the moment you set it; no restart. At 64 the font is the old one to the byte on every cell that existed. exec cells256.cfg / cells64.cfg.
cl_particles_soft
24 by default, a distance in world units; 0 is the old hard edge. Every particle is a flat square facing you, and where one cuts through a wall or the floor the engine has always drawn a hard straight line across it — a smoke puff sliced by a doorway, a fireball half inside the wall it hit, dust that ends in a razor edge on the flagstones. With this set, each particle fades out over that many units as it approaches the surface behind it, so smoke wraps a corner and fire sits in a wall rather than being cut by it. It reads the scene's own depth, which the fog, the HDR path and the upscaler already publish; the muzzle flash over your gun is exempt. exec soft_on.cfg / soft_off.cfg.
cl_particles_refract
12 by default, a strength in pixels; 0 draws the old bright ring alone. The explosion's shockwave ring, and the ball's, are drawn today as a bright expanding ring. With this set, a second ring travels with each that shows nothing of itself: every point on it is the scene behind it, fetched a little further out from the centre, so the wall and the floor and the fog behind the wave visibly bend outward for the fifth of a second the wave takes to pass. It reads the same copy of the frame the water refracts, taken only on a frame with a wave in it. exec refract_on.cfg / refract_off.cfg.
r_skylightning
On by default; 1 is a bright storm, 0 no weather at all. On a map with open sky, every so often (r_skylightning_period seconds on average, 60) the sky lights everything under it with a cold flash from a random high angle, with real shadows, for a few frames (r_skylightning_hold, 0.05 s), flashes again a beat later, and one to four seconds after the thunder rolls in from that side of the sky through the room's reverb. The air lights with it: r_skylightning_fog (0.5) is how much of the flash reaches the fog, carried by one light pushed up the flash's own direction on the thunderbolt's own per-light fog weight. The fog kernel shadow-tests it, so a sealed room stays dark while the air under open sky flashes. Turn it down first if your murk is pale and your gamma bright; 0 is the flash as it first shipped, surfaces only. It needs the ray tracer's wall lighting, as the sun does. exec skylightning_on.cfg / skylightning_off.cfg, with skylightning_fog1.cfg and skylightning_nofog.cfg either side of it.
r_caustics
0.6 by default; 0 is still water and dead stone. The floors and walls of pools take a moving pattern of light cells, the way sunlight through water does, seen through the surface from above or from under it. It reads the same map of the liquids the fog uses, so it needs the liquid fade and the lava boil left at their defaults; r_caustics_scale sizes the cells and r_caustics_speed paces them. exec caustics_on.cfg / caustics_off.cfg.
rt_metal_shadowlights
How many of a pixel's brightest lights get a shadow test. 3 by default; 1 is the old behaviour. Until now the ray tracer shadow-tested exactly ONE light per pixel — the brightest one there — and every other light in range was added with no occlusion test at all. A light in the next room, inside a ceiling recess or behind a pillar therefore painted its full circular pool straight through the wall, which is what a clean round lit patch in an odd place always was. At 2 and above the next brightest lights are shadow-tested too, one after another, with no randomness and nothing to settle over time. The second light costs about a tenth of a millisecond on the ray-tracing stage (roughly one percent of a frame) and the third and fourth are free, because a batch of pixels has already paid for the first. rt_metal_shadowlights_rays is how many rays each of those extra lights spends. exec spotfix_off.cfg is the old behaviour, spotfix_on.cfg the default and spotfix_max.cfg four lights at four rays.
m5_powerupglow
On by default; 0 is 1996's. A player holding the Quad or the Pentagram has carried a plain white light since the original game, and under full wall lighting that white light is bright enough to flatten against the ceiling of the tone curve and end in a hard rim — a spotlight rather than an aura. With this on the Quad glows blue, the Pentagram red and both together magenta, over twice the distance at less than the brightness, so the light falls away gradually and nothing clips. m5_powerupglow_radius is how far it reaches; the value itself is the strength, so 0.5 is half of it. exec quadglow_on.cfg / quadglow_off.cfg / quadglow_soft.cfg.
rt_metal_contact
On by default; 1 is the full effect, 0 the old even softness. Every ray-traced shadow today is as soft as rt_metal_softness says, whether the thing casting it stands on the floor or hangs from the ceiling. With this on, one extra ray per pixel finds how far the blocker is, and the shadow sharpens as the blocker approaches the surface it falls on — a monster's feet crisp on the flagstones, a pillar's shadow hard at its base and soft at its tip. It costs about a fifth of a millisecond on the ray-tracing stage at Best (some two percent of the frame), which is why it is a switch and not a tier setting. exec contact_on.cfg / contact_off.cfg.
rt_metal_fog_liquidlight
On by default; a fraction, 1 is the full effect and 0 the old blind water. The ray-traced fog already works out, step by step, how much of each light scatters through the murk and where the shafts fall, and under water it discards all of it, on purpose, so the underwater picture is the water's own colour and thickness alone. With this set, that light comes through: shafts down into a pool from the torches above it, the murk under water lit where a light reaches it, on top of the water's own colour. The water's density and colour are unchanged. exec fog_liquidlight_on.cfg / _half.cfg / _off.cfg.
rt_metal_gi_ao
On by default; 1 is the full effect, 0.5 half, 0 the old flat fill. The bounce ray that carries the room's own light into its corners already knows how far it went before it hit something, and a short distance is a corner, the foot of a wall, the underside of a ledge. With this set, the flat ambient fill — the little light the ray tracer adds everywhere so that nothing is pure black — darkens in exactly those places, by this fraction, over rt_metal_gi_ao_dist units (64). The direct light and its shadows are untouched. It costs nothing measurable where bounce lighting already runs (Good and above) and does nothing where it does not. exec gi_ao_on.cfg / gi_ao_half.cfg / gi_ao_off.cfg.
cl_particles_scorchglow
On by default. The mark a ball lightning leaves where it earths on a wall, and where a grounding arc lands, and the mark of an axe blow on stone, glow orange at the moment of the strike and cool to nothing over about three seconds, lying flat on the surface over the dark scorch. Rockets do not take it: their explosion carries no surface to lie the glow on. exec scorchglow_on.cfg / scorchglow_off.cfg.
cl_particles_blood_droplet
On by default. The blood in the air is drawn as single hard-edged drops, each at its own angle, instead of the eight random splatter cells Quake has used since 2001, which read as soft red smudges at any size. The blood on the walls is unchanged. exec droplet_on.cfg / droplet_off.cfg.

The atlas cells, for anyone editing m5/effectinfo.txt. Cells 34 to 40 are the fork's own: the shell casing, the dust disc, the shockwave ring, the muzzle flash, the spark streak (38), the ember (39) and the blood droplet (40). The explosion's fast sparks, the ball's sparks and the axe's sparks use the streak; the explosion's slow bouncing embers and the axe's falling embers use the ember; the blob is cell 63, and tex 63 63 on any layer puts it back.

The fog's grain, and what keeps it down. Each fog sample shadow-tests one light, so a sample is either lit or black, and the raw fog buffer is covered in a grain a few fog texels across. Three things hold it: the sampling jitter is spatiotemporal blue noise (rt_metal_bluenoise, on), so the grain reads as featureless and each pixel's sequence over frames cancels rather than piles up; the light test always includes the brightest light so no sample can go fully black (rt_metal_lightsample_hybrid 3, the single-pass form — on every tier that runs the pick, since your eye passed exec a5_both.cfg, with the fog's shadow cast every fourth step); and a depth-aware 3×3 filter runs over the fog at its own resolution before it is magnified (rt_metal_fog_filter 1, every tier). If a fine fizz still shows parked in a lit bank, exec despeckle.cfg runs the filter twice with the second pass spread wider (rt_metal_fog_filter 4, one extra pass) and the two-ray form of the hybrid; exec despeckle_off.cfg restores the shipped picture. A deeper flat history is deliberately not part of it — measured against the true fog, any flat history trails the scene when you strafe — which is what the 3D fog volume above is for.

Fog met a wall's silhouette against sky, or a pillar against the far wall, with a staircase of steps at every tier once. That was the fog buffer's own pitch showing through its magnification, and the fog is magnified depth-aware now (rt_metal_fog_upsample, on by default): at every silhouette each pixel is rebuilt from the fog that was marched to its surface rather than blended with the fog behind it. The interior of every surface is untouched. 0 restores the plain magnification exactly, for A/B.

Stuck? r_volumetric_probe prints what the fog actually sees where you are standing — liquid or not, height above the floor, resulting density, and a vertical profile. It also prints your coordinates, which makes it the viewpos this engine never had.

Where to look

The great hall of e1m2, Castle of the Damned, walking forward through the lit fog with the torches either side — this is where the light pick and the 3D volume earn their keep. Then the lakeside of e1m4, the Grisly Grotto: a cave mouth opening onto water, the mist lying along the waterline, and the torch-lit shrine on the far bank reading dimly through it rather than being erased by it. That view is the reference the whole fog was tuned against.

III · The water

The water

Stand on the bank and look down. The floor of the pool is there, further off than you thought, and the light on it is not the light you brought.

Quake's water was a sheet. It scrolled, it had a colour, and it hid whatever lay beneath it — on the stock maps the engine never even drew the pool floor, because the map's own visibility data never let the air see below the surface. It is water now. It bends what lies beneath it, it ripples at the pace of real water, the murk thickens inside it, and it goes dark at depth the way a lake does: a shallow pool shows you its stones, a deep one shows you nothing, and you are not sure which kind you are looking at until you are in it.

Two switches sit under everything here. Force Water Alpha (r_wateralpha_force, on the Effects and Particles page) makes see-through water real on the stock maps at all — the engine silently ignores Water Alpha there otherwise, because the 1996 map data fails its visibility test — and Water Alpha below 1 (yours is 0.7) makes the surface clear. Everything below wants both.

r_watersurface
On by default. The surface refracts what lies beneath it, the way FTE's water does — measured against FTE running on this Mac, and remade step for step from its shader. Two things make it water rather than a knob. The pool floor is drawn: the visibility of the leaf just below each water surface is merged into the view's, once per pool, so the floor arrives in the frame lit by the ray tracer and fogged by the murk like any floor, and the refraction has something to bend. And the ripple is read off the water texture's own 64×64 level, two layers of it magnified five times and scrolling across each other so that a feature crosses its own width about once a second — the rapid ripple — which means a hi-res replacement pack never changes it, exactly as FTE derives its water from the palette texture. The surface texture wobbles with the classic Quake warp on top. Looking straight down, the water is clear; as your view flattens toward the far bank the texture takes over, by a Fresnel on the rippled normal, so the surface sparkles ripple by ripple. It is all screen space — one copy of the frame per view with water in it, no second scene render — and it costs nothing at all on a frame with no clear water in view.
The knobs are FTE's numbers: _distort 0.1 (“fairly gentle”; 0.2 is big waves), _speed 1, _bump 4, _fresnel 5, _warp 1. Two are ours: _taper (48) fades the displacement to nothing where the floor is within a few feet of the surface, so the water's edge is not smeared out from under it, and _guard rejects a displaced sample that lands on something nearer than the surface, so a torch on the bank is not pulled into the pool. The tint on the refracted view fades with the surface, because far water is already the fog's own pixel and tinting that painted distant pools as green shapes cut out of the murk.
exec watersurface_on.cfg is the shipped look; watersurface_fte.cfg is FTE like for like (crystal-clear, no taper); watersurface_big.cfg the exaggerated version, to see the mechanism; watersurface_off.cfg the flat sheet.
r_watersurface_clear
How thick the in-water murk is when you look into water from the air, as a fraction of the density inside it. At 1 — your word, with the moat in front of you: “1.0 is right. That is perfect” — the murk is as thick from the bank as it is once you are in, so a deep pool hides its bottom and a shallow one reads, with the change eased over a fraction of a second at the waterline either way. 0 gives FTE's crystal-clear water from the bank.
r_volumetric_waterdensity · _slimedensity · _lavadensity
The murk inside. It half-obscures at roughly 70 divided by the value in world units, so 2.5 is a hand's reach — “we really can't see a hand in front of our face”, which is where your config was — and 1.0 is a room's. Your config carries 1.0; a fresh install ships 0.4, clearer still. The ladder is yours: exec uw_clear.cfg (0.6), uw_mid.cfg (1.0), uw_thick.cfg (2.5). Lava has a density of its own (0.4), so raising the water to make swimming murky no longer piles the same murk over a lava lake as an orange haze; slime inherits water at -1 and can be set apart. Each has its own colour, _watercolor_, _slimecolor_, _lavacolor_ red, green and blue.
r_volumetric_liquidfade
Clear water is drawn after the fog, so the fog cannot reach it, and without this a pool at the far end of a foggy hall reads bright and hard-edged in the middle of the murk. This fades the surface into the fog with distance like everything else, and it is on at 0.5: at 1, thick fog fogs the water you are standing beside as hard as the pool across the room, and the near water stops reading as water. 0 for the old picture. The engine says so on the console when you are in the configuration that needs it and have turned it off.
r_volumetric_watermist · _mistheight · r_volumetric_liquidfloor
The lake. A band of mist lies on every liquid surface, 26 units thick at the shipped height (exec mist_low.cfg 12, mist_mid.cfg 26, mist_high.cfg 40; _watermist is the strength, yours 0.69). At 5 it is a skin only the shore ever sees — a ray across the lake never meets it while rays grazing the bank run inside it for a stretch, which is why it once seemed to appear only at the lake's edge. And the surface is the floor of the air above it (_liquidfloor, on): the air murk settles on the local floor, and over a lake the floor beneath a column is the lake bed, so the murk over the water once thought itself hundreds of units up and vanished, while the murk over the bank hung at head height — a hard line along the shore, lit by the torches. Measured on the e1m4 lakeside, the fog over the water is now level with the fog over the bank. The one side effect: the cap measures to the nearest liquid, so a walkway beside a pool takes a little more murk within a cell of the edge. exec liquidfloor_off.cfg shows the old picture. The baked map itself places each water top at the surface now rather than up to a body-length above it, which is what once swallowed the fog beside channels and floated the mist a hand above the lake; nothing to set.
r_waterswirl
The surface churns gently under the fog's own noise on top of the classic scroll, so the sheet stops reading as a sliding decal. 0 is the old look exactly.
rt_metal_liquids · rt_metal_liquids_minlight
RT Liquid Lighting on the RT Shadows page: the ray-traced light multiplied into clear water (yours is 0.45; a fresh install ships 0). One caveat. Liquids are kept out of the shadow structure on purpose, so they cannot block shadows or god rays — which means the light the water takes is the light of whatever lies under it, shadows included, and submerged geometry can print itself onto the surface: on e1m1 the bases of the pillars read clearly through slime you should barely see into. _minlight puts a floor under that light and the shapes go. It is on at 5, so you should never meet the problem; the trade is that a floor that high flattens the effect into a plain brightening, because the printed shapes and the light's variation are the same thing. 3 gives a little more variation for a little more showing through; 0 is the old unclamped behaviour.
Two other answers were built and you sent both back. One light reading per pool (exec liquid_own_on.cfg) showed its seams as large squares over the slime. The honest version — the water's own light at every pixel, with a reflection of the room mirrored off the surface, faint straight down and strong across a lake (exec liquid_rt_on.cfg) — worked, and you decided against it: “I don't think reflections on the water is the way to go.” Both stay as A/Bs, off.
rt_metal_liquidemissive
Opaque water and slime get the lava treatment: the rays stop at the liquid surface, so a murky pool reads as its authored murky texture — machinery and secrets beneath it stay hidden — instead of the pool floor's lighting printing through the sheet. With clear water it stands down automatically and the liquid lighting above takes over.

If you have a replacement texture pack installed — the QRP map textures are — a replacement image for a water, slime or lava texture used to make the engine load it as an ordinary opaque wall, throwing away the fact that it was a liquid: no transparency, no ripple, no boil, no glow, no lava light, and liquids casting shadows they should not. That is fixed and on by default (m5_liquidflags); 0 restores the old behaviour exactly.

What the water costs, measured on a settled machine: about two thirds of a millisecond of GPU where the frame is all water, which comes to 3% of the frame on e1m2's moat, 9–10% in e1m1's slime hall and 5–7% on e2m4 — the drawn floor, the copy of the frame and the surface itself together — and nothing where no clear water is in view. The tier figures further down were taken before the water shipped, so on the maps with water in them they are a few percent high.

Where to look

The start of e1m2 — the pools either side of the bridge, and the moat beyond it with the pillars running down into the water and the far wall's foot showing through. The moat on e1m3, from the bank. The slime at the start of e1m1, the Slipgate Complex, and the moat by its first bridge with a torch on the far wall. Then e1m4's lake, from the cave mouth, for the mist. Then walk in.

IV · The lightning gun

The bolt and the ball

The bolt is a straight line drawn between you and what you have decided to kill. The ball is a decision you have let go of.

There are two ways to spend a cell. Hold the trigger and the thunderbolt is a jagged white line that re-rolls its shape fifteen times a second and burns the room it crosses. Press 9 and a different gun rises in your hands — the plasma gun from Dissolution of Eternity — winds up for half a second with a rising whine, and lets go of a sphere of plasma that drifts down the line you aimed, arcing at everything it can see. It does not care whose side you are on. After a second in the air it will take you as readily as anything else, and it hums while it decides.

The thunderbolt

r_lightningbeam_m5
On by default. The thunderbolt becomes a real discharge: jagged, forked, re-rolling its shape about fifteen times a second, and lighting the room as it goes. It burns brighter and sizzles louder the moment it connects with something, so you know you are hitting. Its colour follows the lightning colour sliders you already have. It holds one overall shape for about half a second and then jumps to another, the way an arc in a plasma globe does, and it is clipped to the world so it never draws through a wall or a pillar. Options → Lightning Gun is its page: World Light is the row to reach for first and Wildness the one that changes its character most, with Branches, Core Hotness, Core Volume and Fog Scatter beside them. 0 gives the stock beam back, exactly.
Its tips behave like the real thing. Every free end — where the bolt lands, and each fork dying in mid-air — frays into finer, dimmer, wilder branches, the last stretch is furred with hair-thin streamers, and the ends dissolve away instead of stopping square on a blunt edge. The previous instant's fray lingers underneath as it fades, so the fine detail shimmers rather than flickering on and off at the re-roll rate. r_lightningbeam_m5_frizzle 0 gives you the old blunt tips.
The beam's edge boils. The bolt's own radius is modulated by a travelling noise, so its width breathes along its length and the edge writhes rather than sitting as a smooth tube — the same idea as the lava's heat shimmer, and noise rather than a sine wave for the same reason. The shipped numbers are the ones you tuned in play: a fine ripple travelling slowly, because the crackle comes from the shape re-rolling and the boil only has to drift. r_lightningbeam_m5_fizz 0 gives the smooth tube back; _fizz_scale sets how long a ripple is and _fizz_speed how fast it travels.
The bolt is drawn as a distance field on Metal, which removes the over-bright cross that used to appear at every kink — worst near the gun, and very obvious in screenshots — and leaves the beam thinner and more sharply defined. r_lightningbeam_m5_sdf_gain makes it brighter or dimmer, ..._sdf_falloff tighter or softer, r_lightningbeam_m5_sdf 0 goes back to the ribbon. And the arc no longer leaves the same prong every time: it wanders between the gun's three electrodes a few times a second, the way an arc really does. r_lightningbeam_m5_muzzlenodes 0 pins it to the right-hand one.
The lighting needs RT Shadows on. Without the ray tracer the bolt is still jagged, but it lights nothing — there is no other route in this engine. The fog glow additionally wants rt_metal_fog. It supersedes the old cl_beams_lightatend, which is console-only.

The ball

m5_balllightning
On by default: a ninth weapon, on key 9, with a Ball Lightning row on the M5 Fun Mods page. Own the thunderbolt and you also own its launcher. Key 9 selects it (impulse 202; press again for the bolt), the engine binds the key for you unless your config has already given 9 to something else, and it spends the same cells, paid at the press.
The flight. The gun winds up for under half a second, a globe growing at the muzzle and a whine rising with it, and the ball leaves the barrel as a bubble a quarter its size and swells to a globe a few feet out. It flies at 400 units a second down the line you aimed — brisk, but a thing you can watch — with a slow sway on its course, for 3.75 seconds. It leans gently toward whatever is near it, more toward a Shambler than a grunt, but it never turns to hunt: it is a dumb ball of plasma, and it goes where you sent it. Its body is small enough to pass any gap you can.
What it does. Ten times a second it zaps everything living it can see within its reach — the damage falling with distance and doubled at point blank — and sets it alight, whatever the Lightning Ignites row says: it is plasma. It draws its arcs at the nearest four, and an arc that lands forks on from its victim to the next living thing the victim can see, two hops out, so a pack dies together. A direct hit is fatal to anything but a Vore or a Shambler, and either way the globe disintegrates: the shell swells and blows apart in a flash, a ring, a storm of sparks and a thunderclap, and a spherical blast of a rocket and a half falls on everyone around the victim. On Quad Damage, which multiplies it by four like every weapon, a direct hit clears the room.
How it ends. When its life runs out, or it touches water, it discharges — a blink, a shockwave ring, sparks, a thunderclap — and ignites whatever survives. A wall or a door does not explode it: it stops there and earths, throwing an arc into the surface with a pop and then another every sixth of a second, sparks and scorches at each, while the shell collapses into the stone. And after one second in the air it is armed against you as well. A standing shot is always clear; chasing it is not. The drone rises in pitch as it comes within its reach of you and a violet cast creeps in at the edges of the screen.
The look. A plasma globe: a dark sun boiling with plasma, ridged cells veined blue-white, turning slowly, rim-lit, three-quarters opaque so that it dims what lies behind it. Fine violet filaments ride its surface and curl out to the glass; the longer it flies the more violently its surface heaves, the filaments lifting off as prominences and the eruptions throwing sparks; when an arc lands the core flares and one or two thick filaments take the charge, like a hand on the glass. Its arcs are plasma filaments too. It carries a light in the bolt's own hue and flicker that scatters in the fog like the bolt's, the fog shrouds it as it would a torch, and it leaves a short blue-white comet. Metal draws the sphere; the GL path draws a knot of strands.
Every number is a m5_balllightning_* console cvar — speed, life, reach, damage, cost, the blast, the lean, the charge, the fizzle, the arming second, how many arcs it draws and how far they chain — and the globe's own radius, palette and edge cast are r_lightningbeam_m5_ballsize, _ballcolor_* and _ballpressure. exec ball_v2.cfg is this ball; ball_v1.cfg the slower, gentler one from the first day, for A/B; ball_off.cfg removes the weapon entirely. The Weapon Kick row gives it a slow heave. The glow it trails and the burst it dies in are blocks in m5/effectinfo.txt, tunable live with cl_particles_reloadeffects.
Where to look

The skill hall of the start map, from the top of the stair: fire a ball down it and watch it drift over the pads, then chase it and learn why you should not. The start of e1m2, where a pack is waiting at once, to chain through. And a horde wave on dm4 (m5_horde 1, impulse 210) with a fresh ball every refire.

V · The mods

The mods

The old rules bend here — the guns kick, the dead burn, the waves keep coming — and the game beneath does not object. It has seen worse.

The gameplay half lives on Options → M5 Fun Mods, each mod its own switch, so the whole page can be put back to the 1996 guns from the menu. The thunderbolt and the ball have the chapter above; the handlamp is here because you carry it.

m5_shotgun
A Doom shotgun: ten pellets, tighter spread, harder kick, a bigger warm muzzle flash and ejected shells. The shell is a real shotgun hull — red body, brass head — that tumbles as it flies, bounces and comes to rest, instead of the round spark the engine has always drawn (the spin was always there, but a round dot looks the same at every angle). m5_shotgun_casing 0 brings the round spark back.
m5_muzzleflash
On by default since 2026-09-16, on your word once the flash and the sparks had both passed. Quake has never drawn a muzzle flash: the flash is a light and nothing else, so with the fog on it reads as a soft glow round a barrel that shows no flash of its own. With this on, the shotgun, the super shotgun, the nailguns and the two launchers get a starburst with a hot core at the muzzle, read off the weapon's own animated mesh each frame so it follows the barrel through its kick, gone in about seventy milliseconds, drawn over the weapon and brighter than white on the float scene buffer; every monster's gun gets a smaller one as it fires. The same switch makes the sparks off a bullet strike thin hot streaks instead of the round blob stretched into wide yellow rays, and the bullet strike's grey blob a small burst. The thunderbolt and the ball are their own flash. m5_muzzleflash_size scales it, _forward and _up nudge it in world units; exec flash_off.cfg is the 1996 picture, the light alone, and flash_on.cfg puts it back. On the nailgun the flash alternates between the two barrels with the shots, always on the barrel that fired. Your verdict on the flash: "the flash is fine", the rocket launcher's too.

The shotgun and the super shotgun also throw a spray of hot streaks from the barrel tip — eight a shot, fourteen from the super shotgun — that travels with the gun, so it stays on the barrel when you strafe, turn or bob, and each streak points out along the barrel. It is drawn like the flash, over the weapon. It has seven knobs, each a scale on the spray as it ships: m5_muzzleflash_sparks is how many (0 for none; the flash and the shells stay), _speed how fast they fly (faster sparks also streak longer), _spread how wide they fan, _length and _thickness the streak itself, _life how long they last and _brightness how bright they start. exec sparks_reset.cfg puts all seven back. The spray needs m5_shotgun on.
m5_torch_embers
3 by default, a rate; 0 puts the flames back to their 1996 stillness. Every torch, brazier and candle in view sheds that many embers a second — hot grains that rise out of the flame, drift, cool from orange to a dull red and go out within a second or two. Only torches you can see spawn them, and never more than a handful a frame. exec embers_on.cfg / embers_off.cfg.
m5_dust
Dust in the air. Quake has never had any — every particle it draws is fired by something. Set this to a count (128–512 is the range) and that many motes drift and eddy in the air around you, spawned only in open air you can see, recycled as you move. Each mote is a crisp point, not a blob. Since 19 September they are flecks of bonfire ash — sixteen weighted colours, soot and charcoal through charred brown, a warm and a cool grey and slate blue, with one off-white in sixteen — where they used to be an even grey ramp from soot to pure white. That ramp is why they read white, and the reason is arithmetic rather than taste: it is a uniform population, so 57% of motes sat above mid-grey against 12% on the palette, and the mean mote was 143 of 255 against 82. m5_dust_tint 0 is the old ramp back. They are lit like any other particle, so turn on cl_particles_lighting 2 with it: motes catch torchlight, hang in a doorway's light and flare when a rocket goes past. If they still catch your eye too much, the knob is cl_particles_lighting_static — it dims the light they take from the room's own torches while leaving muzzle flashes, explosions, rocket glow and the handlamp at full strength, so the dust sits dark in a corridor and still flares the instant something goes off. exec dust_ash.cfg is the tuned set, dust_ash_dark.cfg the rung below it and dust_white.cfg the old look exactly. The count is absolute — it does not ride the particle-quality multiplier — and the emitter stands aside when an explosion needs the particle pool.
m5_burn
The lightning gun sets things on fire — flame, smoke, burn damage, and the victim charring as it goes. The fire carries a real dynamic light, so a burning monster lights the fog and casts ray-traced shadows. A Shambler's bolt sets you alight too. The ball lightning ignites regardless.
m5_venom
The Scrag's spit reads as acid — Alien rather than fireworks. A yellow-green light rides the projectile, so it lights the wall it is passing and the fog it is passing through, and casts real shadows on the way. Its trail burns instead of sitting flat, and its impact sizzles: vanilla drew nothing whatever when a spit hit flesh.
Weapon feel
Five rows, each its own switch: four ship on, and the grenade bounce is off. Weapon Kick gives every gun a kick of its own instead of the stock two degrees — a snap from the shotgun, a heavier one with the view shoved back from the super shotgun, a tremor from the nailguns, a lob from the grenade launcher, a real shove from the rocket launcher, a buzz from the thunderbolt, a slow heave from the ball gun; the slider is the strength, 1 as designed; your verdict, "weapon kick feels fine". Grenade Bounces gives the bounce a random pitch and a volume that follows the impact, and a dull tap once it is barely rolling — and your ear rejected it ("it sounds like a xylophone"), so it stays off and feel_on.cfg sets it back to the stock bounce. Nail Tracers puts a short hot streak behind every third of your own nails, so a burst shows in fog where a bare nail does not; m5_nailtracer_every sets how often (5 for every fifth, 1 for every nail). Nails From Barrels lines the nailgun's two alternating streams up with its two barrels, so each nail appears at the tip of the barrel that fired it, where the flash is. id started each nail a little wide of its barrel, so on screen the streams seemed to leave from outside the gun ("they were too wide before"). The nails still hit where id's did. The super nailgun keeps id's single centre line. Axe Sparks throws sparks and a ricochet ring off a wall the axe hits ("fine"). Your verdict on the set was "perfect. feel_on is good. make that default", so the kick, the tracers, the barrel streams and the axe sparks are on by default, with the stock bounce. exec feel_off.cfg puts all five back to the 1996 guns; feel_on.cfg brings the shipped set back.
Explosions and teleports
Quake explosions have never had smoke. They do now: a white blink, a bang and an expanding shockwave ring that blinks the room's light with them, a fireball with real structure rather than a smooth ball, then — a beat later, so you see the fireball first — hot brown smoke rolling up close in and a slower grey plume boiling above it, embers that fall and bounce, and a scorch mark. Teleports lose their cloud of three thousand grey dots in favour of a hundred and forty-five streaks and motes — which is a speed change as much as a look one, because a teleport fires on every respawn and three times for every monster a horde wave spawns. The 1996 sprite — the flat orange fireball Quake has always drawn — is gone by default: it was the last part of an explosion with a hard bottom edge against any surface not seen head on, and the one part the fog could never reach (drawn after the fog, no depth), so in a thick corridor it stayed bright and sharp while the new fireball and smoke sat in the murk at their own distance. m5_explosion_sprite 1 brings it back.
This lives in m5/effectinfo.txt, which is data, not code. Edit it and type cl_particles_reloadeffects in the console to see the change immediately — no rebuild, no restart. Rename the file to get the old effects back.
torch — the bad torch
A handlamp you carry: warm tungsten, a soft-edged cone, and thoroughly unreliable. It grumbles constantly, swoons to a third of itself about every twelve seconds, and roughly every minute nearly dies — a second of sullen ember-red before it catches again. It reddens as it dims, the way a cooling filament really does. The beam trails your view slightly, as though swinging in your hand, and drifts when you stand still. It can never go out: the floor is built into the code, not into a setting, because a lamp that reaches zero is a strobe — and that stays true whatever you set the two brownout timings to. Its edge falls off over more than half its width, so it reads as a beam on a wall rather than a stencilled disc, and it scatters in fog strongly enough that the shaft of it is visible in the air and not only the patch where it lands.
There is no menu row — it wants a key. Options → Customize Controls → torch (handlamp), or type bind f torch. An engine default cannot beat your saved config, so it has to be bound once by hand. The beam, its shadows and its scatter through fog all come from the ray tracer on your settings; m5_torch_cone takes it from spot to lantern and m5_torch_softness decides how hard its rim is (0.35 is a disc-like edge, 1 a gradient all the way to the axis). m5_torch_filament (0–1) shows the bulb: a hot core about a third of the beam across with a faint dark ring outside it, like a cheap reflector imaging its filament, on the wall, in the fog and in the bounce alike. m5_torch_dip_period and m5_torch_swoon_period are the two brownout timings in seconds.
m5_horde
Wave survival on any map. With notarget on, the monsters ignore you and fight each other instead. Horde Director, the row beneath it, lets the horde read you: at each wave's start it weighs your health and armour, the ammo for the guns you own, how fast you cleared the last wave and how badly it hurt, and shapes the next one — a smaller wave of rabble and a longer breather after one that nearly had you, a bigger wave with more of the heavies and a shorter break when you are fat, and a supply drop that grows when you need it. impulse 211 shows what it made of you. Off by default, so the fixed waves of before are one click away.
m5_bullettime · m5_photomode · m5_movement · m5_gore
Hold a key to ease time down; freeze the world and fly around with the HUD hidden; Quake 3 air control or easy bunnyhopping; and gore from vanilla up to ludicrous.
m5_noclipfly
On by default: noclip flies where you look — forward follows your view up and down at full speed, the way Quake Enhanced does it. Set it to 0 for the classic level-flight noclip.

VI · More Quake

The mission packs

Beyond the four episodes there are other dimensions, and they were built by other hands.

Single Player → Mission Packs switches between Quake and the five add-ons: Scourge of Armagon, Dissolution of Eternity, Dimension of the Past, Dimension of the Machine and Arcane Dimensions. It takes effect immediately — no restart, no command line. Anything not installed is greyed out and marked absent.

The MetalQuake content comes with you: the higher-poly models and the map textures still apply, and every engine feature in this guide — the ray tracing, the fog, the water, the thunderbolt — is unaffected, because none of it lives in a game directory. The gameplay mods do not, because a mission pack brings its own game code and replacing it would break the pack. Those rows grey out while a pack is loaded and tell you why.

The packs each carry their own lighting, and they used to arrive looking nothing like each other — Armagon far too dark, Dimension of the Machine sun-baked and glowing orange. Most of that was a genuine bug: modern map compilers write light colours on a 0–255 scale and this engine read them as 0–1, so a modern map's lights arrived up to 255 times too bright and strongly tinted. With that corrected, each pack is levelled towards Quake's own brightness while keeping the colour its mapper actually chose, and a map that asks for its own fog now gets one fog rather than two.

Each pack has a small text file of its own — m5/m5pack_hipnotic.cfg and its siblings — holding just the handful of settings that differ for that pack. They are meant to be edited; delete one and that pack simply runs uncorrected.

Dimension of the Machine's horde maps will load but not play: that mode lives in the rerelease's own engine, not in the map.

One local tweak ships with Arcane Dimensions: its own config turns on the mod's developer helper markers — translucent direction arrows and little spinning diamonds scattered around path corners, teleports and monsters — because this engine keeps developer mode on for its diagnostics. They are switched off in ad/quake.rc. If you ever reinstall AD from a fresh download you will see them again; the fix is one line and it is written down in docs/CONTENT.md.

Arcane Dimensions is a different beast from the mission packs: a full total conversion with its own weapons, monsters, breakable scenery and secrets, and its own custom HUD (which works here). Because it replaces the game code wholesale, the M5 gameplay mods are off inside it by construction — but everything engine-side still applies: the ray tracing lights its candle halls, the murk takes each map's own fog colour, the enhanced thunderbolt still fires, and it is the one place with a real sun to switch on. Its start map is a hub; walk into a portal to pick a level. Two tips: the key inventory is impulse 175 (AD suggests binding i to it — bind it yourself if you want it, it is deliberately not bound for you), and its maps are the biggest this engine runs, so if a frame ever feels heavy, the M5 Quality row is the lever.

VII · Stillness

The view that never quite settles

Stand still long enough and the view begins to drift, as though the thing behind the eye were breathing.

Stand still for a second or two without moving the mouse and the view begins to wander — the same slow drift the end-of-level camera has. Touch anything and it snaps back out inside a quarter of a second, so it never fights you.

Quake shipped this and then switched it off. The three sine terms that produce the drift have been in the code since 1996, driven by a value called v_idlescale that nothing in the engine ever set. Original Quake used it in exactly one place — it forced the intermission camera to drift — and even that was lost along the way. Both are back. Options → Idle View Sway turns it off if you would rather stand perfectly still.

It moves the view, never your aim: the drift is added to the camera, not to the angles the server sees.

VIII · Darkness

How dark it should be

Between the torches the black is honestly black. It is not empty.

Quake is a game about not being able to see. The stock brightness controls could not reach the bottom of that — the menu floored gamma at 0.5 and contrast at 1.0 while the engine itself allows far lower — so Options → Brightness and Gamma leads with a single Brightness slider that covers the whole range.

SliderWhat you get
0%Almost unplayable. Inky black; you can barely make anything out.
15%Very dark and gloomy, inky corners and shadows.
25%Moody and atmospheric, but comfortably playable if you know the map.
50%The 1996 look — and exactly no change at all.
100%Clearer and more lit than Quake ever was.

It scales the gamma curve, so it dims the whole finished frame rather than just the lighting. That is deliberate: the fog's colour is authored rather than lit, so dimming the lighting alone would leave the fog looking like it was glowing on its own. The three rows under the slider — gamma trim, contrast, scene brightness — are trims for a particular display, not the main control.

If your blacks still will not go dead, the last thing holding them up is rt_metal_ambient (0.15), a flat fill that stops surfaces facing away from every light going pure black under wall lighting. It is light with no source, on purpose.

IX · Brightness

Letting the highlights off the leash

A muzzle flash, for a single frame, brighter than the glass was built to show.

Everything above is about how dark the game can go. This is the other end. On a display running in macOS HDR mode, Extended Dynamic Range hands a muzzle flash or a pool of lava to the screen brighter than white, instead of flattening it to the same white the health digits are already at. The HUD deliberately stays at ordinary white while the scene runs past it — so the two side by side in one frame are the test.

It is one switch: the HDR (EDR) row on Options → Video Options, or r_edr 1 in the console. Everything it needs — the float scene buffer, the analytic gamma curve — it sets up for itself (about 8% of a frame, and a one-off shader-rebuild pause the first time).

The test is in the frame itself: the HUD stays at ordinary white on purpose, so a muzzle flash or a glow should read clearly brighter than the health digits beside it. (The desktop dimming when it takes hold is sometimes quoted as the tell — on this rig it does not, and the effect works regardless, so judge it by the game.) r_hdr_shoulder 0.75 alongside it is worth having: it rolls the brightest parts smoothly up into the new range instead of letting them pile up at the top (a value of 1 is clamped to 0.99, which is effectively off — 0.75 is the knee to use). If the last few bright specks on monster edges bother you, r_fxaa 1 in the console (there is no menu row) softens them at a small cost in texture crispness. r_edr 0 puts everything back exactly.

The bloom is the other half of a highlight, and the engine's own is the 2001 one: the scene shrunk to a small buffer, squared a few times and blurred with offset quads, with no threshold and no chain of sizes, so every bright thing becomes a wide haze rather than a hot core with a glow. r_bloom_m5 1 (with r_bloom 1, the master and its menu row) replaces it: a soft threshold on the float scene buffer at half resolution, five halvings down through a thirteen-tap box, and a tent upsample summed back up, added by the same composite as before. A muzzle flash, a lava pool or the core of a bolt gets a tight bright core and a glow that falls off across the room instead of a smear. Your six bloom knobs are the old chain's and are ignored while it is on; r_bloom_m5_threshold (1: only what is brighter than white), _knee (0.5: how softly it engages), _intensity (0.35) and _levels (5: how far the glow reaches) shape this one. exec bloom_m5_on.cfg / bloom_m5_off.cfg; it takes the old chain's place rather than adding to it, and it costs about a tenth of a millisecond more than the old blur at your settings (+3.7% of the renderer's GPU time, measured by toggling the two in one boot). Off by default until your eye has passed it.

If nothing happens, type r_edr_report. It says in one sentence which requirement is missing, and then prints what headroom every attached display is actually giving you.

A screenshot cannot show you any of this, and neither can a screen recording — there is no above-white information in an image file, so both come back looking exactly like the ordinary picture. It has to be judged on the glass.

X · Performance

One control for the lot

The machine has a temperature and a mood, and every number below was taken when it had settled into both.

Options → M5 Quality cycles seven tiers across thirty-four settings at once. Since the 19 September retier every tier but Stock renders at 37.5% and traces the ray-traced light at exactly that size — pixel for pixel with the raster — and antialiases the result at full size after the upscale. That pairing is what removes the jagged silhouettes, and it is cheaper than what it replaced: Ultimate used to render at 1920×1080 and magnify a 720×405 traced term onto it, and now renders 720×405 and traces 720×405, which is the same trace with a raster 7.1× smaller. Paired and measured on demo48, three rounds in alternating order, the new Ultimate is ×1.50–1.61 of the old one on the average and ×1.36–1.42 on the worst second.

Ultimate is exec rich.cfg wired into the menu — your own 19 September verdict, "it looks best (i.e. sky jaggies gone) if I select Ultimate in the menu, and then exec rich.cfg", in one click. Better, Best and Ultimate use the temporal scaler with its reactive mask; Superfast, Fast and Good use the spatial one — Superfast went back to spatial on the 19th, because the reason it had been moved to temporal the day before ("spatial cannot reconstruct a silhouette") stopped being true once the antialiasing moved to full size. Bounce lighting is on for Best and Ultimate only.

Jagged silhouettes — SOLVED on 19 September, and the cause was not the render scale. You reported jagged edges where an object meets the sky, and where something near meets something far — worst on Superfast, still visible on Best. The first diagnosis blamed the render scale and the upscaler. It was wrong, or rather it was half the story: what ramps a silhouette is the ray-traced term being magnified onto the raster. Under wall lighting that term is the scene lighting, and a magnified term smears each step over about two rows — which is precisely the shape a morphological antialiaser cannot see. On your own aaroof.dem the staircase measures 0.362 untreated, 0.384 with r_smaa alone (worse), 0.318 with a raster-exact term alone, and 0.229 with both. Neither half gets there; the pair does.

Every tier now ships that pair, so there is nothing to switch on. r_fxaa_post is superseded by r_smaa and both it and r_fxaa are pinned off by the tiers — r_fxaa runs at the render resolution, where at 37.5% it only softens what the scaler is about to work from. If you want the old render-resolution FXAA back, exec fxaa_on.cfg; the M5 Quality row will then read Custom, honestly. One trap worth knowing, because it cost a session: exec nofx_aa.cfg appeared to do nothing when run on top of Ultimate. It asks for the spatial scaler so that r_smaa has a full-size target — but the spatial scaler is refused at Render Scale 100%, which is what Ultimate had, so there was no scaler, no target, and the pass silently did not run. A refused pass draws a perfectly correct frame and says nothing.

The second filter, and the pairing it needs. FXAA corrects at most half a pixel, and only near a step's ends — which is exactly why your steep A-frame leg came out “super smooth” and the shallow lit band came out “jagged af”. The leg steps every 3 pixels and is inside the filter's reach; the band steps every 6–18 and is not, and widening the reach past about 24 makes every slope worse rather than better. r_smaa 1 runs a morphological pass instead: it walks each step to both its ends and blends every pixel along it by that pixel's own coverage, which is a ramp across the whole tread rather than a nudge at its ends. Against an analytic edge whose right answer is known it takes a 1:6 staircase from 0.340 to 0.008, where FXAA at span 8 reaches 0.230.

It has one condition, and on your own frames it is the condition that decides everything. A morphological pass needs a step. Soften that step over two or three rows and it can no longer tell which row the boundary is on, so it correctly declines to act — the same 1:6 edge, ramped over three rows, goes only to 0.230. Your band's edge is ramped: its largest single-row step carries 0.48 of the transition's contrast where a clean step carries 1.00. What softened it is the ray-traced light, which Ultimate draws at 600×337 and enlarges 3.2× to fill the screen — and under wall lighting that light is the picture. Draw it at full size with rt_metal_scale 1 and the pair reads 0.229 on your own demo against 0.362 untreated and 0.283 for the best FXAA. Neither half gets there alone. The term at full size costs frames, so the pair is a trade rather than a free win; exec aa_term1_smaa.cfg is it, aa_smaa.cfg is the pass alone, and aa_reset.cfg puts your saved settings back. Both default off until you have judged them.

Every row above was measured on one afternoon, on one machine state, on the same five maps, with Good, Better, Best and Ultimate re-taken the evening the fog verdict landed — so the rows really are comparable with each other. Three things to know about the numbers. They are hot-state figures: the machine is roughly a third faster for its first few minutes and then settles, and the same demo run fourteen times in a row falls from 148 fps to 111 and stays there. The 1-second minimum is a single worst second, so it wanders more than the average does. And two things have shipped since the table was taken, in opposite directions: the water costs a few percent on the maps with water in them, and the cheaper single-pass form of the fog's light test with a cast every fourth step gives Best and Ultimate a few percent back; the next settled bench squares it.

Read it as bands: Best is the full look — temporal smoothing, no fog or shadow crawl, bounce lighting on, and every light casting a shadow in the fog — and it clears 100 fps on every map measured. Better keeps that look and runs about a fifth faster on its own smaller fog and ray-tracing buffers. Good trades the temporal smoothing and the bounce light for a big jump in rate; Fast runs the same ray-traced fog on a smaller buffer (the depth-aware upsample keeps its edges clean) and can push a high-refresh panel hard; Superfast goes further still, trading render resolution and per-light fog shadowing for a rate that saturates a 240 Hz panel on every map measured. At the other end, Ultimate is the antialiasing tier: it is the only one that renders at native 1920×1080, which turns the temporal scaler from an upscaler into pure antialiasing, and it traces the lighting at 720×405. The 1-second lows on the busier maps are set by the game's own heavy moments as much as by these settings, and on the heaviest there is a floor no setting can beat: on e3m1, with the ray tracer, the fog, bloom, FXAA, the heat shimmer and the red glow all switched off, the 1-second floor still only reaches 176. Prefer native rendering on another tier? Set r_viewscale 1 in the console — the row then reads Custom.

Two switches that cost nothing and are kept for the record. exec asskip.cfg (the default) stops the ray tracer rebuilding its picture of the monsters and torches on frames where none of them moved — a fifth to a third of frames in ordinary play, every frame when you stand still with no torch in view; the picture is identical to the byte. exec pipeline.cfg lets the ray tracer and the ordinary scene draw run on the graphics chip at the same time instead of one after the other; they do, and the frame comes out the same length, because the chip is already fully busy and the work is only reordered. pipeline_off.cfg is the default there.

The upscaling itself: Render Scale picks how much of the window the scene renders at (67% renders 44% of the pixels), and MetalFX Upscale hands the scaling to Apple's scaler instead of plain bilinear, which on its own looks soft. With it on, the stonework comes back — measured at 85–106% of the native frame's edge energy against bilinear's 62–70%. The HUD and console stay sharp either way, and it composes with HDR. The M5 Quality tiers set both for you; the two rows on Options → Video Options are where you override it by hand. The MetalFX row greys only when the renderer is not Metal; it cycles Off → Spatial → Temporal, skipping Spatial at Render Scale 100% because a 1:1 spatial upscale is a pointless copy.

Temporal upscaling is what the top three tiers use. Instead of scaling one frame cleverly, it accumulates several frames that were each rendered a fraction of a pixel apart — which is aimed squarely at the crawling mesh you could see in thick fog, rather than at saving pixels. Measured against the spatial scaler on a frozen foggy view, the fog's frame-to-frame crawl drops by a factor of four or five, and the standing grid halves in strength — because the spatial scaler had been sharpening the mesh along with the stonework. It costs 22–25% of the frame rate at the same Render Scale, and the crawl fix is the same at 67% as at 100%, so there is no reason to raise Render Scale for it. You can cycle the MetalFX row by hand, or set r_metalfx 2 at the console.

Two honest caveats. Anything that moves without telling the scaler how — a rocket's glow sweeping across a floor that is not itself moving, the smoke it leaves, blood and sparks — can leave a faint trail; the rocket itself, and monsters, doors and lifts, report their own motion and should not. r_metalfx_reactive 3 (which taa_on.cfg sets) tells the scaler to distrust its history exactly where moving light and particles land, and measured against the scaler's own "forget everything" mode it removes nearly all of that lingering; what is left is the temporal scaler rendering small bright puffs a touch softer than bilinear does, which is a look and not a trail. And you lose the spatial scaler's extra bite on the stonework. r_metalfx 1 puts you back exactly where you were; exec taa_on.cfg and exec taa_off.cfg switch between the two without typing, and r_metalfx_reactive_debug 1 shows you what the scaler is being told (white = distrust).

The ray-traced shadows have their own resolution knob and the two multiply — at r_viewscale 0.667 a rt_metal_scale of 0.375 traces at a quarter of the window. Raise rt_metal_scale if shadow edges read soft under the scaler.

XI · Replay

Watching it back

Nothing in a demo is live, and it will still make you flinch.

The engine can be made to watch itself. Two ways: a showreel that plays your own demos and switches the fork on one feature at a time, and a capture that runs a demo far past any tier's settings, one fixed sixtieth of a second per frame, however long each frame takes.

The showreel

exec showreel.cfg at the console and sit back: about nineteen minutes, no input, sound on, run it on Metal. It plays your own demos for their themes and adds the fork's features one at a time, about every twenty-five seconds, in the order they were built — the 1996 picture on e3m1, then the modern baseline, the red glow, the murk, the ground fog, dust, ray-traced shadows, full wall lighting, ray-traced fog, bounce lighting, the upscaler and HDR; then the fog in motion on e1m2 (the light pick, the 3D fog volume, the handlamp); the lava on e1m7; the thunderbolt and the ball lightning on the start map; water on e1m2 and in the Elder World (opaque, clear, murk inside, fading into fog, ray-traced light); the lake on e1m4 (the mist band and the water as the fog's floor); the room reverb and the occlusion low-pass on e2m4; and a finale that runs four action demos through timedemo, each at the Stock tier and then at Best, with the engine's own fps line left on screen after each. Every label sits top-left and the fps meter bottom-right, so nothing covers the thing being shown. It ends by re-reading your config, so nothing it touched survives. exec showreel_ab.cfg is the older four-minute reel that switches each feature off and on against one scene.

The cinematic capture

A picture no tier can play, for a video. A capture does not run in real time: the engine advances the demo a fixed sixtieth of a second per frame however long each frame takes, so every history, every jitter and the fog's own clock run by the frame count and the result is the same every time you run it. That lets the ray tracer be set far past any tier: exec cinematic.cfg puts the trace at native resolution with 32 shadow rays a pixel, the fog at a native buffer with 64 slices and a shadow cast in every one, the light pick at two rays a cast, bounce lighting at full rate, and the upscaler at native (where it is pure antialiasing), then sets the capture to 60 frames a second. Then:

playdemo demo11
cl_capturevideo 1

In that order: starting a demo stops the one already playing (the attract demo counts), and a demo stopping switches the capture off. The capture stops itself when the demo ends and the file lands in your m5/video/ folder. Ogg (Theora) is used when the libraries are installed and a plain uncompressed AVI otherwise — large (about 180 MB a second at 1080p60), so keep captures short or set cl_capturevideo_width 1280. QuickTime will not open the raw AVI (it reads it and calls it unplayable): hand the file over and it is converted to a QuickTime .mov (HEVC, with the sound) with the fork's own converter (test/avi2mov.swift), or let ffmpeg compress afterwards. Run exec cinematic_off.cfg (or click your quality tier) before you quit: most of these are archived settings and would otherwise be what your config saves. Sound is captured with the video. Two captures of the same demo agree to the frame but not to the pixel: the dust, the particles and the upscaler’s history are random by design. If a cut needs two identical takes, add m5_dust 0, cl_particles 0 and r_metalfx 1 for both.

XII · Sound

The room answers

You hear the room before you see it.

Three things, built to one rule: Quake's samples are the sound. With them off every sample plays exactly as it always has, to the bit; with them on the sample stays the loudest thing you hear, and what is added is what the room does to it — a crypt that rings, a door that muffles, a distance that dulls. Stereo only; music never goes through any of them.

snd_reverb
On by default: every room has a voice. The engine looks around you ten times a frame — up, down and eight ways along the floor — to see how big the space is and how much sky it can find, and sets a reverb to match: a crypt rings for a couple of seconds, a corridor is tight, open ground is nearly dry, cross-fading over half a second as you walk through a door. Far sounds are wetter than near ones; your own gun sends a little. snd_reverb_wet (0.15 — halved on your ear: “a bit too glossy” at 0.3) is the level and snd_reverb_decay (1) scales how long rooms ring. exec reverb_off.cfg restores silence behind the samples.
snd_occlusion_lowpass
On by default: a sound that a wall is between you and is dulled, not only turned down — so a Shambler behind a door reads as a muffled presence rather than as a distant one. snd_occlusion_cutoff (1200 Hz) is how muffled. exec occlusion_lp_off.cfg reverts.
snd_airabsorb
Off, for your ear: exec airabsorb_on.cfg takes the top end off far sounds the way air does. Nothing within about three hundred units changes at all, and from there a sound dulls gently with distance until, two thousand units out, an explosion is the low thud you would hear across a valley. The value is the strength (1 = the whole fall), snd_airabsorb_dist where it completes, snd_airabsorb_cutoff how dull it gets. An outdoor map is the place to judge it — e1m1's start with a fight in the distance, or the Arcane Dimensions hub. exec airabsorb_off.cfg reverts.

The soundtrack

Drop the CD tracks in as track02.ogg, track03.ogg and so on — either in id1/music/ or id1/sound/cdtracks/ inside your DarkPlaces folder. Each map asks for its own track as it loads, so nothing needs typing: music simply starts. To test one by hand the command is cd play 2 — and it must be 2 or higher, because track 1 on a Quake CD is the game data, not music. Volume is bgmvolume, separate from sound effects.

If the music is silent while sound effects are fine, the Ogg decoder is the thing to suspect rather than the files. The engine loads it at startup and says so — look for Loaded library "libvorbisfile.dylib" near the top of the log. It also looks in Homebrew's folder, so music no longer depends on how the game was launched.

XIII · Quick start

If you change five things

Five things, and then go down.

rt_metal 1 and r_volumetric 1 light up the two big strands if your config does not already carry them. Set M5 Quality to taste — Best is the reference look. Then, on the Volumetric Fog page, keep Air Density low — around 0.2 — and let corner gathering do the work; fog that looks right in a corridor is usually far too thick in a hall.

Switch on Force Water Alpha on the Effects and Particles page and set Water Alpha below 1, or the water stays a sheet and nothing in its chapter can reach it. The thunderbolt is on by default and has its own Options → Lightning Gun page — World Light first, Wildness for its character — and the ball is on key 9.

The menus were rationalised and then halved: every slider that survived was measured to actually change the picture, and the rest went to the console (SETTINGS.md lists them all). A greyed row means the setting will not change anything in your current configuration — each page prints the reason at its foot, and a greyed row does not move its setting at all. The old one-shot preset rows are gone — M5 Quality on the Options page is the one preset, and it only touches performance.

On an HDR display, r_edr 1 is the one switch for above-white highlights — it sets up everything it needs itself. The Brightness chapter above has the detail.

XIV · Another Mac

Taking it with you

It travels in two pieces, and neither is any use without the other.

The game ships as two things that live side by side, in any folder you like — inside Applications, in a Games folder, on an external disk:

QuakeM5.app
packs/id1/PAK0.PAK …
packs/m5/ …            the mods
packs/hipnotic/ …      and rogue, dopa, mg1, ad

packs holds the game data. The app looks for that folder next to itself, so the pair can be moved anywhere together — but the app on its own will not find anything to run. To add a mission pack later, drop its folder into packs; to remove one, delete it and the menu simply lists it as absent.

To build the two zips, from the repo:

sh release/make-release.sh

That produces a small QuakeM5-app-….zip (a few megabytes, everything the engine needs bundled inside — no Homebrew or other install required on the other machine) and a large QuakeM5-packs-….zip holding the game data. They are separate on purpose: the data rarely changes, so once it is on the other Mac only the app zip needs sending again.

First launch on a new Mac — do this before anything else. macOS quarantines anything that arrives by download or AirDrop, and that causes both failures you might see: the app refusing to open at all, or opening but insisting "the required files were not found" while packs sits right beside it. The second one is macOS secretly running a hidden copy of the app from a temp folder (App Translocation), so it genuinely cannot see its neighbours — the app's own error screen says so when it happens. One Terminal command fixes both, once: open Terminal, paste xattr -dr com.apple.quarantine (with the trailing space), drag the QuakeM5 app onto the Terminal window, press Enter. This is macOS being careful about an app not signed by a paid developer account, not a fault in the build.

Your settings come too

The packs zip carries packs/m5/config.cfg — your settings — and a machine that has never run the game adopts them as its defaults. The moment you quit, the game writes that machine's settings to its own home folder instead, and from then on the shipped copy is ignored. So it is a starting point, not a lock: to get back to it, delete the local copy and start the game again.

To move just the config later, without rebuilding anything — quit the game on both machines first, because it rewrites this file when it exits and a copy pasted while it is running is lost on quit:

  1. In Finder press Shift-Command-G and paste ~/Library/Application Support/darkplaces/m5
  2. Copy config.cfg
  3. On the other Mac open the same folder the same way, and paste, replacing what is there. If the game has never run on it, run it once and quit so the folder exists.

Screen settings live in that file too — it asks for 1920×1080 exclusive fullscreen. On a Mac whose display differs, set the resolution again in Options → Video; everything else carries over untouched.

XV · Honestly

What it does not do

Light bounces once here and no further. Some things stay in the dark because we have not yet found the way to them.

The ray tracer composites over a rasterised frame, and it is not a path tracer. Light bounces once and no further: bounce lighting fills shadowed corners with the colour of the room around them, but a second bounce, caustics and mirror reflections are all outside what this does.

The water reflects nothing. A reflection was built — the room mirrored off the surface, weighted the way real water weights it — and you decided against it, so the water refracts and does not reflect, and that is a choice rather than a limit. The sun is an option, not a default, and the fog beneath it takes no daylight yet. The plasma globe is drawn as a sphere on Metal only; the GL path shows a knot of strands in its place.

The fog's baked map is 64 units per cell, so a ledge narrower than that can be missed. Air fog carries no lighting of its own unless the ray-traced fog is on. The MetalFX upscaler's temporal mode (r_metalfx 2, above) is off in a bare configuration and selected by Better, Best and Ultimate; its known trails — a moving light on still geometry and the particles of smoke, blood and sparks, neither of which any motion vector can describe — are what the reactive mask (r_metalfx_reactive) is for.
